# The Muslim Brothers and Force and Revolution

Some people ask: Will the Muslim Brothers use force in achieving their goals and reaching their end? And do the Muslim Brothers think of preparing a general revolution against the political system or the social system in Egypt? I do not wish to leave these questioners in perplexity; rather, I seize this opportunity to lift the veil from the explicit answer to this with clarity and plainness, whether they like it or not.

As for force, it is the slogan of Islam in all its systems and legislations. The Noble Qur'an calls out with clarity:

> "And prepare against them whatever you are able of power and of steeds of war by which you may terrify the enemy of Allah and your enemy." [Qur'an, Al-Anfāl 8:60]

And the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "The strong believer is better than the weak believer." Indeed, force is the slogan of Islam even in supplication, which is the manifestation of humility and submission. Among what he used to supplicate for himself, and teach his Companions, and address his Lord was:

> "O Allah, I seek refuge in You from grief and sorrow, and I seek refuge in You from incapacity and laziness, and I seek refuge in You from cowardice and miserliness, and I seek refuge in You from being overcome by debt and overpowered by men."

Do you not see in this supplication that he sought refuge in Allah from every manifestation of weakness: weakness of will by grief and sorrow; weakness of production by incapacity and laziness; weakness of body and wealth by cowardice and miserliness; weakness of might and dignity by debt and subjugation? What, then, do you expect from a human being who follows this religion except that he be strong in every matter whose slogan is strength in everything? The Muslim Brothers must be strong, and must act with strength.

But the Muslim Brothers are deeper in thought and farther in vision than to be seduced by the superficiality of works and thought without diving to their depths, examining their results, what is intended by them, and what is wanted through them. They know that the first degree of strength is the strength of creed and faith; next, the strength of unity and connection; then after that, the strength of arm and weapon. A group cannot be described as strong until it is fortified by all of these together. If it uses the strength of arm and weapon while its limbs are dislocated, its order disturbed, its creed weak, and its faith dormant, its fate will be annihilation and perdition.

This is one consideration. Another: Did Islam — while force is its slogan — command the use of force in every circumstance and condition? Or did it set limits for that, stipulate conditions, and direct force toward defined goals? A third consideration: Should force be the first remedy or the last cauterisation? Is it not a person's duty to weigh the beneficial results of using force against its harmful results and the circumstances that nullify its use? Or is it his duty to use force and then accept whatever comes? These are considerations that the Muslim Brothers weigh before adopting a style of using force. Revolution is the most violent manifestation of force; the Muslim Brothers look at it with the utmost precision and depth, especially in a land like Egypt which has tried its luck with revolutions and reaped nothing but what you know.

After all these considerations and assessments, I say to these questioners: The Muslim Brothers will use practical force where there is no other way, and when they are certain that they have completed the equipment of faith and unity. When they use this force, they will be honest partners, they will give warning first, wait afterwards, then proceed with dignity and might, accepting with satisfaction and contentment all the consequences of this position of theirs.

As for revolution, the Muslim Brothers neither call to it, rely upon it, nor believe in its benefit or purity. Yet they will frankly tell every government in Egypt that if affairs continue in this manner and those in charge do not think of urgent reform and swift treatment of these problems, that will lead to a revolution that is not the work of the Muslim Brothers and is not from their call, but from the pressure of circumstances and the demands of the situation — an indication of reform, not a call to it. These problems that grow more complicated with time and more severe by the day are but one of these warnings. Let the reformers hasten with action.
