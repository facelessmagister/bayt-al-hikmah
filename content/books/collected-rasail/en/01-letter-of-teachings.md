# Epistle 1: The Letter of Teachings (Risālat al-Taʿālīm)

In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful. Praise be to God, and prayers and peace be upon the Imam of the God-fearing and the leader of the strivers, our master Muḥammad, the trustworthy Prophet, and upon his family, his Companions, and whoever follows him until the Day of Judgment.

To proceed:

This is my letter to the striving brothers of the Muslim Brotherhood, who have believed in a noble *daʿwah* (دعوة) and a pure idea, and who have resolved sincerely either to live for it or to die in its path. To these brothers alone do I address these brief words; they are not a lesson to be memorized, but teachings to be acted upon. So to action, O sincere brothers! God, His Messenger, and the believers will see your deeds, and you will be returned to the Knower of the unseen and the witnessed, and He will inform you of what you used to do. "And indeed this is my straight path, so follow it, and do not follow the paths, for they will separate you from His path." That is what He has enjoined upon you, that you may become righteous. [Qur'an, Al-An'am 6:153]

As for those other than these, they have their lessons, lectures, books, articles, demonstrations, and administrations, and each has what he is inclined toward. So race to forgiveness from your Lord and a Garden whose width is that of the heavens and the earth. [Qur'an, Al-Hadid 57:21]

And peace be upon you, and the mercy of God and His blessings.

Ḥasan al-Bannā

## To the Sincere Brothers

The pillars of our *bayʿah* (بيعة) are ten; so preserve them:

> Comprehension (*al-fahm*), devotion (*al-ikhlāṣ*), action (*al-ʿamal*), *jihād* (جهاد), sacrifice (*al-taḍḥiyah*), obedience (*al-ṭāʿah*), steadfastness (*al-thabāt*), resoluteness (*al-tajarrud*), brotherhood (*al-ukhuwwah*), and trust (*al-thiqah*).

---

### The Twenty Principles of Understanding Islam

O sincere brother, know that our idea is a genuinely Islamic one, and that the understanding of Islam as we understand it, within the limits of these brief principles, is all-encompassing: it is state and homeland, government and nation; it is might and mercy, justice and culture; it is law and judgment, science and judiciary; it is wealth and prosperity, earning and richness; it is *jihād* and *daʿwah*, army and thought; and it is true creed and worship.

1. The Noble Qurʾān and the pure Sunnah are the reference for every Muslim in understanding the rulings of Islam. The Qurʾān is understood according to the rules of the Arabic language, without affectation or excess, and by referring the established Sunnah to trustworthy men of *ḥadīth*.

2. Sincere faith, sound worship, and spiritual struggle have a sweetness and delight that God casts into the heart of whichever of His servants He wills. But dreams, inspirations, unveilings, and visions are not proofs of legal rulings, and are not considered valid unless they do not conflict with the rulings of the religion and its texts.

3. Amulets, incantations, charms, magic, fortune-telling, claiming knowledge of the unseen, and everything of this sort is forbidden and must be combated — except what is a verse of the Qurʾān or a transmitted supplication.

4. The opinion of the Imam and his deputy in matters where there is no explicit text, and where there are several possible interpretations and compatible interests, is to be followed so long as it does not clash with a legal principle. It changes according to circumstances, custom, and interests. In acts of worship, the outward form is repeated without regard to meanings; in customary matters, attention is paid to secrets, wisdoms, and objectives.

5. Everyone's words may be accepted or rejected except the infallible one [the Prophet], peace and blessings be upon him. Whatever comes to us from the predecessors — may God be pleased with them — that agrees with the Book and the Sunnah, we accept; otherwise, the Book of God and the Sunnah of His Messenger are more worthy of being followed. But we do not harbor resentment against individuals over matters of difference; we leave them to their Lord, for they have conveyed what they were entrusted with.

6. Every Muslim who reaches the level of examining the evidence of subsidiary rulings should follow an imam from the people of religion, provided that he strives as much as he can to know the evidence, accepts every guidance accompanied by proof once it is valid in his view, and relies on it even if his own legal ability is insufficient, until he reaches the level of independent examination.

7. Juridical disagreement in subsidiary matters is not a reason for division in religion, nor does it lead to enmity or hatred. Every *mujtahid* has his reward, and there is no objection to a sincere layperson examining matters of disagreement in the light of love for God's sake and cooperation in reaching the truth, without this leading to blameworthy disputation or fanaticism.

8. Every issue that yields no benefit is from the affectation that we are religiously forbidden to engage in. This includes excessive subdivisions of rulings that do not occur, delving into the meanings of the noble verses of the Qurʾān that scholars have not reached, and discussing what happened among the Companions — may God be pleased with them. There was disagreement among them, but each has the merit of his companionship and the reward of his intention, and there is room for interpretation.

9. Knowledge of God — exalted is He — belief in His oneness, and belief in His most beautiful names and sublime attributes, and their sound narrations, together with what resembles that of ambiguity, we believe in as it has come, without interpretation (*taʾwīl*) or divestment (*taʿṭīl*), and without delving into what the scholars have differed about. Sufficient for us is what sufficed the Messenger of God, peace and blessings be upon him, and his Companions. "And those firmly grounded in knowledge say, 'We believe in it; all is from our Lord.'" [Qur'an, Aal-E-Imran 3:7]

10. Every innovation in the religion of God that has no origin in it, which people have approved out of caprice — whether by addition or diminution — is misguidance that must be combated and eliminated by the best means that do not lead to what is worse than it.

11. Additional innovations, acts of scrupulosity, and strictness in unrestricted acts of worship are juridical disagreement; every scholar has his opinion, and there is no objection to examining the truth with evidence and proof.

12. Love of the righteous, respect for them, and praise of them in accordance with the known measure of their closeness to God — exalted is He — and the saints mentioned in His saying, "Those who believed and feared Allah," are affirmed with their legal conditions, while believing that they — may God be pleased with them — have no power to bring benefit or harm to themselves in their lives or after their deaths, let alone to anyone else.

13. Visiting graves, in whatever form, is a legislated Sunnah with a transmitted manner. But seeking help from those buried, whoever they may be, calling upon them, asking them to fulfill needs from near or far, vowing to them, building over graves, covering them, lighting lamps at them, wiping oneself upon them, swearing by other than God, and whatever is connected with that of innovations — all of this is forbidden and must be combated, and we interpret these actions as means that lead to that.

14. Supplication, when combined with seeking access to God through one of His creatures, is a subsidiary disagreement in the manner of supplication, not a matter of creed.

15. Erroneous custom does not alter the realities of legal words; rather, one must verify the intended meanings and avoid verbal deception in every aspect of religion. The consideration is with intentions, not with mere words.

16. Creed is the foundation of action, and the action of the heart is more exalted than the action of the limbs; both are required by the Sharīʿah, even if the level of demand differs.

17. Islam honors the intellect, urges reflection upon the universe, elevates the status of knowledge and scholars, welcomes the beneficial good from every source, and holds that wisdom is the lost property of the believer; wherever he finds it, he is most worthy of it.

18. Both legal and rational examination may address matters outside the sphere of the other, but they cannot conflict on definitive matters. A true, general fact will not clash with an established legal principle. The probable from either is interpreted in favor of the definitive. If both are probable, legal examination is more worthy of being followed until the rational matter becomes definitive or is refuted.

19. Do not declare a Muslim an unbeliever who affirms the two testimonies, acts upon them, and performs the obligations — out of opinion or disobedience — unless he utters a word of disbelief, denies a necessarily known matter of the religion, openly rejects the Qurʾān, interprets it in a way the Arabic language cannot bear under any circumstance, or acts upon something that permits no interpretation other than disbelief.

When the Muslim knows his religion in these principles, he knows his slogan forever: "The Qurʾān is our constitution, and the Messenger is our exemplar."
