# The Muslim Brothers and the Egyptian Constitution

Some people also ask: What is the position of the Muslim Brothers toward the Egyptian Constitution? Our brother Salīd Afandī ʿAshmāwī, editor-in-chief of the magazine *an-Nadhīr*, has written on this subject, and the newspaper *Miṣr al-Fatāt* dealt with his article critically and comparatively. This is a good opportunity for me to speak to you about the opinion of the Muslim Brothers and their position toward the Egyptian Constitution. Before that, I would like to distinguish clearly between the *constitution*, which is the general system of government that defines the limits of authorities, the duties of citizens, and the extent of their powers over governments; and the *law*, which regulates the relations of individuals with one another, protects their personal and civil rights, and holds them accountable for their actions. After this explanation I can clarify for you our position toward the constitutional system of government in general, and toward the Egyptian Constitution in particular.

The fact is that the Muslim Brothers hold that when a researcher looks at the principles of constitutional government — which are summarised in preserving personal freedom in all its forms, consultation, derivation of authority from the Ummah, responsibility of rulers before the people and their accountability for their actions, and clarification of the limits of each authority — these principles appear to him to agree completely with the teachings, systems, and rules of Islam, in a precise form.

Therefore the Muslim Brothers believe that the constitutional system of government is the system of government in the whole world closest to Islam, and they do not prefer any other system to it.

After that remain two matters: first, the texts in which these principles are cast; and second, the method of application by which these texts are interpreted. A sound, strong principle may be placed in an ambiguous text that leaves room for play, in spite of the soundness of the principle in itself. And an apparently clear text of a sound principle may be applied and executed in a way that follows caprice and desires, so that it is nullified. This application may nullify every benefit expected from it.

If this is established, then among the texts of the Egyptian Constitution there are, in the view of the Muslim Brothers, ambiguous texts that leave wide room for interpretation and explanation serving ends and caprices; these need clarity, determination, and elucidation. This is one. The second is that the method of executing the Constitution and attaining the fruits of constitutional rule in Egypt is a method whose failure experience has proven, and which has brought the Ummah harm rather than benefit; it is in dire need of alteration and modification of its intentions, in the extreme.

We think it right to point here to the electoral law, which is the means of testing the deputies who represent the Ummah and carry out and protect its constitution. What this law has brought upon the Ummah of conflicts, rivalries, and manifest harms is witnessed by observable reality. There must be enough courage to face these mistakes and work to correct them.

For this reason the Muslim Brothers work so that the ambiguous texts in the Egyptian Constitution may be defined and the method by which this Constitution is applied in the land may be reformed. I think that the position of the Muslim Brothers has become clear by this explanation, and matters have been returned to their rightful place. Our brother Salīd Afandī ʿAshmāwī, in his first article, wished to express the duty of criticism the Muslim Brothers had made, and he became heated and intense. We do not deny that this is not our actual position, for we accept the fundamental principles of constitutional government, considering them in agreement with, indeed derived from, the system of Islam, and we criticise the texts and methods of enforcement. He wished to express that and put the matter in its sensational position with respect to the Muslim Brothers, so he made light and slighted. In both positions he is rewarded for his intention, and good intention is better than [mere] knowledge. We thank those who took our brother Salīd Afandī to task for this lapse, but I do not think he will be angry at this reproof, for moderation in every condition is best. I think there is no room for speech after this explanation. As for detailed examples, abundant proofs, and descriptions of methods of treatment and reform, they are in a special epistle, Allah willing.
