# Epistle 6: The System of Government in the Light of the Islamic System

The Way of the Word

## 1. The System of Government

> "And judge, [O Muḥammad], between them by what Allah has revealed and do not follow their inclinations and beware of them, lest they tempt you away from some of what Allah has revealed to you." [Qur'an, Al-Māʾidah 5:49]

### Government in Islam

Upright Islam presupposes government as one of the foundations of the social system it brought to humankind. It does not approve of chaos, nor does it leave the affair to any of its followers: "If you settle in a land in which there is no authority, then leave it." And in another ḥadīth he likewise said to some of his Companions: "If you are three, appoint one of you as your leader."

Whoever thinks that religion — or, more precisely, Islam — has nothing to do with politics, or that politics is not among its concerns, has wronged himself and wronged the public concerning this Islam. I do not say he has wronged Islam, for Islam is the law of Allah; falsehood cannot approach it from before or behind. It has been said in the words of Imām al-Ghazālī, may Allah be pleased with him: "Know that the Sharīʿah is the root and kingship is the guard; what has no root does not endure, and what has no guard is wasted." Thus the Islamic "state" does not stand except upon the basis of "the call" (*daʿwah*), so that it becomes a "state of message," not resembling an administration or a material government, rigid and deaf, without spirit — just as the "call" does not stand except in a life that protects, spreads, conveys, and upholds it.

Our first error was that we forgot this root, and separated religion from politics in practice, even though in theory we could not deny it. We wrote in our constitution that the official religion of the state is Islam; but this text does not permit politicians and leaders of the political arena to corrupt the "Islamic taste" in minds, the Islamic outlook in souls, and the Islamic condition in situations by their belief, declaration, and knowledge in a way that constantly widens the distance between the guidance of religion and the requirements of politics. This is the first step to weakness and the root of corruption.

### The Pillars of Islamic Government

Government in Islam rests on well-known, established principles; they are the foundational pillars of the Islamic system of government. They are: (a) the responsibility of the ruler; (b) the unity of the Ummah; (c) respect for its will. Beyond that, names and forms have no significance.

The ruler is responsible before Allah and before the people; he is their servant and worker for them. The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "Each of you is a shepherd and each of you is responsible for his flock." And Abū Bakr, may Allah be pleased with him, said when he assumed authority and ascended the pulpit: "O people, I used to work for my children and consumed their sustenance; now I work for you, so set aside for me from the public treasury." By this he explained the theory of the social contract in the best and most just explanation; indeed, he laid down its basis. It is nothing but a contract between the Ummah and the ruler to care for public interests: if he does well, he has his reward; if he does ill, he has his reckoning.

### The Unity of the Ummah

The Islamic Ummah is one, because the brotherhood that Islam has joined in hearts is a root of the roots of faith. Faith is not complete without it, nor is it realised without its existence. That does not prevent freedom of opinion and offering sincere advice from the young to the old and from the old to the young — which in the language of Islam is called giving good counsel, commanding what is right, and forbidding what is wrong. The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "Religion is sincere counsel." They said, "To whom, O Messenger of Allah?" He said, "To Allah, to His Book, to His Messenger, to the leaders of the believers, and to their common people." He also said: "If you see my Ummah afraid to say to the oppressor, 'You are an oppressor,' then it has lost its blessing," and in another narration: "The inside of the earth is better for them than its surface." And he said: "The master of martyrs is Ḥamzah ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, and a man who stood up to a tyrant ruler, commanding him and forbidding him, and was killed for it."

Division in essential matters cannot be imagined in the Islamic Ummah, because the social system that unites it is one system — Islam — acknowledged by all its sons. Disagreement in subsidiary matters does no harm, and does not necessitate hatred, enmity, or partisanship that revolves with one's whims... but it does require research, scrutiny, consultation, and sincere advice. What has a definite text admits no independent reasoning; what has no text is decided by the ruler with the community of the Ummah. Nothing beyond that.

### Respect for the Will of the Ummah

The Islamic Ummah must watch the ruler with the closest watchfulness and advise him with what it sees as good. He, in turn, must consult it, respect its will, and take what is sound from its opinions. Allah commanded the rulers in that, saying:

> "And consult them in the matter." [Qur'an, Āl ʿImrān 3:159]

He praised the believers with it, saying:

> "And whose affair is [determined by] consultation among themselves." [Qur'an, Ash-Shūrā 42:38]

The Sunnah of the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, and the rightly guided, rightly acting caliphs after him confirmed this: whenever an affair came to them, they gathered the people of opinion among the Muslims and consulted them.

They yielded to the soundest of their opinions, and they even called them to that and urged them to it. Abū Bakr, may Allah be pleased with him, said: "If you see me upon truth, then help me; and if you see me upon falsehood, then correct me or rise against me." And ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb said: "Whoever sees crookedness in an imām, let him straighten it."

The Islamic system, in this, is not concerned with forms or names, provided these essential principles — without which government is impossible — are realised, and provided they are applied in a way that preserves the balance among them and does not let one overwhelm another. This balance cannot be preserved without a living conscience and a true sense of the sacredness of these teachings, and by guarding and protecting them lies success in this world and salvation in the Hereafter. In modern terminology this is called "national consciousness," or "political maturity," or "civic education," or similar expressions; but all of them return to one reality: belief in the soundness of the system and awareness of the benefit of preserving it. For texts alone cannot uplift a nation, just as a law is of no use unless applied by a just, upright judge.

In our modern life we have imported from Europe this parliamentary system under whose shadow our governments now live. We wrote a constitution on its basis, changed that constitution once under the same name, and experienced many of its effects. To what extent, then, does this system agree with Islam? And to what extent have we benefited from it throughout this period?

## 2. The System of Government

> "Then is it the judgement of [the time of] ignorance they desire? And who is better than Allah in judgement for a people who are certain [in faith]?" [Qur'an, Al-Māʾidah 5:50]

I said in the previous section that the pillars upon which the system of government rests are: (a) the responsibility of the ruler; (b) the unity of the Ummah; (c) respect for its will.

This system was realised in the most perfect form in the era of the rightly guided caliphs after the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him. They felt this with the fullest feeling, and it appeared in all their many sayings and deeds. It is enough for you to read what ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, may Allah be pleased with him, said when he assumed the caliphate, and what ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz said when he likewise assumed authority — their two biographies correspond to his saying: "O people, I have been placed in authority over you, and I would not have accepted it had I not hoped that I would be the best of you for you, the strongest of you over myself, and the most heedful of you concerning what is entrusted to me. O people, I am only one of you; I am not better than any one of you..." [cited from ʿAlī al-Ṭanṭāwī, vol. 1, p. 25].

[ʿUmar was] sorrowful as he awaited the settlement of accounts, taking your rights as you chose and placed them, and I place them, and in travelling among you as you travel. My only help is in Allah. For ʿUmar, when morning came, had no power or strength except in Allah, unless Allah aided him by His mercy, support, and assistance. He used to say: "If a camel perished on the bank of the Euphrates, I would fear that Allah would ask me about it, O Banū al-Khaṭṭāb."

ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz said in his sermon: "As to what follows: there is no prophet after the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, and no book after the Book that was revealed upon him; rather, what Allah has made lawful remains lawful until the Day of Resurrection, and what Allah has made forbidden remains forbidden until the Day of Resurrection. I am not one who restricts, while to me every path is open; I am not an innovator, but a follower; no one is to be obeyed in disobedience to Allah; I am not the best of you, but I am one of you — only Allah has made me a heavier burden upon you than you." When the vehicles of the caliphate were brought to him after the burial of Sulaymān ibn ʿAbd al-Malik, he ordered them to be delayed, mounted his mule, and returned to his field. His freedman Muzāḥim entered upon him and said, "O Commander of the Faithful, perhaps you are overwhelmed?" He replied, "With the like of this affair that has befallen me I have been overwhelmed: there is no one of the Ummah living in east or west but has a right upon me that I must discharge to him, except the writer of my needs and the seeker of my audience."

The Ummah was united in word by holding fast to the fringes of religion, believing in the superiority of the rulings it brought, and observing the effect of the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, and his insistence on unity — to the point that he threatened with death whoever separated from the community or turned away from obedience, saying: "Whoever kills while the community is united... strike him with the sword, whoever he may be." And he said: "Whoever leaves obedience and splits from the community dies a death of ignorance; whoever fights under a banner of blind partisanship, or calls to partisanship, or supports partisanship, and is killed, is killed with a death of ignorance. Whoever goes out against my Ummah striking its righteous and its wicked, not sparing any believer, nor keeping the covenant of one who has a covenant, is not of me and I am not of him."

Their will was likewise respected and valued. Abū Bakr did not present any matter to the people except after consulting them, especially in matters for which there was no scriptural text. The same was true of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb: he made the caliphate after him a consultative matter among the six with whom the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, was pleased when he died.

I also said in the previous section that we imported this parliamentary system, under whose shadow our government lives, from Europe. To what extent does it agree with Islam? And what have we gained from it since it was applied in our country in the last era until now, a period of roughly a quarter of a century?

1) *ʿamiyyah*: i.e. a banner of sedition and ignorance.
2) *ʿaṣabiyyah* of a man means his relatives. *manā*: fighting, calling, and supporting, not for the sake of religion and truth, but purely out of bigotry for one's people and whim.

### The Position of Islam toward the Parliamentary System and the Egyptian Constitution

Constitutional jurists say that the parliamentary system rests on the responsibility of the ruler, the authority of the Ummah, and respect for its will, and that there is nothing in it preventing the unity of the Ummah and the uniting of its word. Division and disagreement are not a condition of it, even though some say that one of the pillars of the parliamentary system is party politics. But this, if it is a custom, is not a fundamental principle of the system, for the system can be applied without this partisanship and without violating its original principles.

Therefore, there is nothing in the principles of this parliamentary system that conflicts with the principles Islam laid down for the system of government. In this respect it is not far from the Islamic system nor alien to it. In this respect we may also say with confidence that the basic principles on which the Egyptian constitution rests do not conflict with the principles of Islam, and are not far from the Islamic system nor strange to it. Indeed, the framers of the Egyptian constitution, although they drafted it on the most modern and refined constitutional principles and opinions, took care that none of its texts should collide with Islamic principles. They are either explicitly in harmony with them, as the text stating, "The religion of the state is Islam," or susceptible of an interpretation that makes them not conflict with them, as the text stating, "Freedom of belief is guaranteed."


## The System of Government (continued)

I would like here to point out the difference between the constitution and the [ordinary] laws that are enacted upon it; for many of these laws — we shall treat each in its place, Allah willing — are not in conflict with what Islam laid down for the system of government. We say rather that there is a shortcoming in the wording of the constitution, an error in its interpretation, and a failure to protect the basic principles that Islam brought and on which the constitution ought to have been founded; all of this led to the corruption of which we complain and the confusion into which we have fallen in all these parliamentary matters.

We shall treat this briefly in a number of aspects.

### The Responsibility of the Ruler and the Ministry

As for "the responsibility of the ruler," the original principle in the Islamic system is that the person responsible is the head of state, whoever he may be: he has the right to act, but he must render account of his conduct to the Ummah. If the head of state is unable personally to exercise this authority and bear this responsibility, it is permissible for him to delegate it — as was done in ministries of delegation (*wizārat al-tafwīḍ*) in many Islamic states. The jurists granted dispensations and permitted this so long as it served an interest; the governing principle in such matters is safeguarding the public interest.

Al-Māwardī said in his book *al-Aḥkām al-Sulṭāniyyah* (الأحكام السلطانية):

> "The ministry is of two kinds: a ministry of delegation and a ministry of execution. As for the ministry of delegation, it is that the Imām appoints someone to manage affairs according to his own judgment and to ratify them in accordance with his own ijtihād; there is no doubt about the permissibility of this ministry. Allah the Exalted has narrated from Mūsā, peace be upon him: 'And appoint for me a minister from my family — Hārūn, my brother; strengthen me through him and let him share my task.' If this was permissible in prophethood, it is a fortiori permissible in the imamate, because what is entrusted to the Imām in managing the Ummah he cannot carry out in its entirety without assistance. The appointment of ministers who share in administration is sounder in the execution of affairs than his exclusive conduct of them, which might make him more prone to error and less secure against failure."

The original principle of this responsibility in the parliamentary system is that the responsible party is the ministry, and no responsibility lies upon the head of state. This is what the Egyptian constitution and the English constitution have adopted; each of them explicitly declares the responsibility of the ministry and absolves the head of state of all responsibility, regarding him as infallible and his person as inviolable.

Yet there is no objection in the parliamentary system to the head of state bearing responsibility and the ministry being regarded as subordinate to him in that; this is what the constitution of the United States of America has laid down. It is strange that the books of Islamic jurisprudence also point to this arrangement, calling it the "ministry of execution" (*wizārat al-tanfīḍ*). Al-Māwardī said in his book *al-Aḥkām al-Sulṭāniyyah*:

> "As for the ministry of execution, its authority is weaker and its conditions fewer, for the final decision rests with the Imām and his own management. This minister is an intermediary between him and the subjects and governors; he conveys the Imām's commands, executes what he has decreed, and carries out what he has decided."

There is no doubt that this demonstrates the richness, flexibility, and perennial suitability of Islamic jurisprudence for every time and place.

### The Ambiguity of the Egyptian Constitution

These, then, are the principles of the Islamic and parliamentary systems together concerning "the responsibility of the ruler." What have we done in Egypt? We have stopped halfway, in both text and application. Our constitution in this respect is ambiguous, concise, unclear, and lacking in detail — even though it is the most important point in defining the form of the political and Islamic life we desire. To explain this, I shall quote what Dr. Ibrāhīm Madkūr, member of the Egyptian Senate, and Mr. Murīq Ghālī wrote in his memorandum *Mizān Jadeed* (ميزان جديد) under the title "The Constitution and Its Ambiguity":

> "As for the first factor, we may sum it up by saying that our constitution, despite its precision and careful wording, has fallen into the same ambiguity that has befallen other constitutions older than it. It has left the most important point in the parliamentary system without sufficient definition: the authority of the ministers and their connection with the people as represented in their deputies on one side, and their supervision of the services required of them through the interests and administrations on the other. Out of excessive brevity it has failed to clarify their position toward the head of state and the king of the country, and has contented itself with phrasing that may be applied to whatever is desired of it.
>
> "It is clear that the ministry is the backbone of the entire parliamentary system, the link between legislation and execution, and the source of movement and life in a system that seeks to respect the authority of the people while conducting the affairs of the state with sound and swift dispatch. When we turn to the constitution alone, we find that what it contains connected with this sensitive point scarcely exceeds three lines, all of them general and vague. Article 29 states: 'The executive authority is exercised by the King within the limits of this constitution.' Article 81: 'The King exercises his authority through his ministers.' Article 84: 'The King appoints and dismisses his ministers.' Article 76: 'His council of ministers is responsible for the affairs of the state.' Article 83: 'The ministers are jointly and severally responsible before the Chamber of Deputies for the general policy of the state, and each one of them is responsible for the business of his ministry.' Article 84: 'Orders of the King — whether verbal or written — do not absolve the ministers from responsibility under any circumstances.'"

Those are nearly all the texts connected with this subject; and we do not think that they suffice in any way to solve any of the problems mentioned. The two authors went on to explain and elaborate in detail what has been summarized above. The important point is that this matter — the very core of the question — needs clarification and stability; it is the first principle of the principles of the "Islamic system" and the parliamentary system alike. Without that, affairs cannot be set right or run smoothly.

## 3. The Unity of the Ummah

> "And judge, [O Muḥammad], between them by what Allah has revealed and do not follow their inclinations; and beware of them, lest they tempt you away from some of what Allah has revealed to you. And if they turn away — then know that Allah only intends to afflict them for some of their sins. And indeed, many among the people are defiantly disobedient. Then is it the judgement of [the time of] ignorance they desire? And who is better than Allah in judgement for a people who are certain [in faith]?" [Qur'an, Al-Māʾidah 5:49-50]

### The Unity of the Ummah and Partisanship

As for the unity of the Ummah, I have already explained that upright Islam presupposes it and regards it as an essential part of the life of the Islamic community; it does not permit any negligence in this. For it regards unity as a concomitant of faith: "The believers are but brothers," just as it regards disagreement and division as a kind of disbelief. Allah the Exalted says:

> "O you who have believed, if you obey a party of those who were given the Scripture, they would turn you back, after your belief, [to being] unbelievers." [Qur'an, Āl ʿImrān 3:100]

That is, after your unity, they would turn you into disbelievers by making you divided. The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "Do not revert after me to disbelief, striking one another's faces." Thus he used the word "disbelief" to express division, disagreement, and mutual striking of faces.

I believe that every parliamentary system — whether Islamic or non-Islamic — does not refuse this unity, especially when the social basis is one in its fundamentals and general directions, as is the case with all Islamic nations together in this respect. Partisanship, division, and disagreement are inseparable from this parliamentary system in Europe and elsewhere, for it arose upon its ruins, and the recurring bloody conflicts between peoples and their rulers are the clearest proof of that. With the diversity of inclinations and differences of opinion... but the Islamic Ummah, from the day of its inception, Allah has protected it from all of that and safeguarded it, through the unity and forbearance of Islam, from this confusion and disturbance.

Nevertheless, the parliamentary system in its oldest homelands is not based upon this excessive partisanship. In England there are only two parties that alternate in power, and its partisanship is almost purely internal, gathering around important national questions; so it does not have a harmful effect. Likewise America has only two parties, and we hear nothing of them except at election times. Countries that have developed partisanship and indulged in multiplying parties have reaped the bitter consequences in both war and peace; France is the clearest example of that.

If this is so, and if the unity of the Ummah is a foundation of the Islamic social system and is not refused by the parliamentary system, then it is fitting that we hasten to unity after partisanship has devastated the land and progeny of Egypt.

### The Egyptian Parties

There is consensus that the Egyptian parties are the greatest evil of this homeland, the foundation of the social corruption by whose fire the people now suffer. They are not genuine parties in the sense understood in any country of the world; they are no more than a series of splits produced by personal disputes among a number of the sons of this Ummah, who were compelled by circumstances at a certain moment to unite in their name and demand their national rights. There is also consensus that these parties have no programs and no methods, and that there is no disagreement among them in anything at all except personalities. Evidence of this is clear in what they announce in their advertisements outside Parliament and in the rhetorical speeches they deliver inside it. If the parties are the ones who present the shaykhs and deputies, and the ones who steer the helm of government in parliamentary life, then it is self-evident that government cannot be sound while those who hold the helm are in such a state.

This statement, upon which the consensus of the Ummah has been established, has been declared openly and clearly by shaykhs, deputies, jurists, and constitutionalists. Whoever reads what ʿAḥwabah Bāshā wrote in his book *Mabādiʾ Waṭaniyyah* (مبادئ وطنية), or what al-Ustādh Ḥasan al-Jadāwī wrote in his book *ʿUyūb al-Ḥukm fī Miṣr* (عيوب الحكم في مصر), or what others among the writers have written, will see the truth of what we say. We think it useful to quote here a passage from the book of the constitutional jurist al-Ustādh ʿAbd al-Ṣabūrī, *Mabādiʾ al-Qānūn al-Dustūrī* (مبادئ القانون الدستوري), concerning the Egyptian parties. He said:

> "The fact is that most of the political parties in Egypt have no program for which their supporters contend. Indeed, every party has become nothing more than a former minister with his partisans and followers. Because of this result, elections will not be a comparison between programs — for these have become identical for all — but rather a matter of personal confidence or comparison between individuals. The elections will be personal, not party elections, in the sense understood among Western peoples. It is obvious that the continued existence of parties in this manner divides the country into factions and groups, and spreads discord and disputes among individuals and families without a comprehensible reason or a sound basis."

If we add to this that Egypt is still, up to the present, an occupied country, and that the only ones who benefit from this division are the occupying foreigners; and if disagreement — which is in any case unpalatable — is disliked in any nation among nations, then the Ummah of the Nile Valley is the most in need of the medicine of unity to gather its strength in the struggle for independence and in the work of internal reform. The matter is too grave to be neglected or treated half-heartedly.

### The Solution for the Egyptian Parties

If this is so, we do not know what can oblige this oppressed, struggling, generous people — these groups and factions that call themselves political parties — to continue. The matter is extremely grave. The reformers have tried to arrive at unity, even if temporary, in the face of the harsh circumstances through which the country is passing, but they have despaired and failed. The matter is no longer tolerable for half-measures. There is no escape now from dissolving these parties altogether and gathering the strength of the Ummah into a single party that works to complete its independence and freedom, lays down the principles of internal reform, and then allows events afterwards to show the people ways of organization within the unity that Islam imposes.

## 4. Respect for the Will of the Ummah: The Electoral System

> "The only statement of the believers when they are called to Allah and His Messenger to judge between them is that they say, 'We hear and we obey.' And it is they who are the successful." [Qur'an, An-Nūr 24:51]

As for respect for the will of the Ummah and the obligation of consulting it and allowing it to participate in government soundly, Islam does not require the expression of the opinion of every individual in every matter — which in modern terminology is the general referendum (*istiftāʾ ʿāmm*) — but in ordinary circumstances it is content with "the people who loosen and bind" (*ahl al-ḥall wa-l-ʿaqd*). It does not restrict them to a specific number or to particular persons. The apparent meaning of the jurists' descriptions of them indicates that this term includes three categories:

1. The jurists relied upon for legal opinions and derivation of rulings.
2. People of experience in public affairs.
3. Those who possess leadership or headship among the people, such as tribal chiefs, family heads, and leaders of groups.

All of these together may be included under the term "the people who loosen and bind."

The modern parliamentary system has arranged the means of reaching these people through the systems of elections and their various methods, which constitutional jurists have devised. Islam does not reject this organization so long as it leads to selecting the people who loosen and bind; that is easy to achieve if any defects are observed.

### Defects of the Electoral System in Egypt

In Egypt we adopted the system of direct election at one time in the law of 1923, and the system of two-stage election at another time in the law of 1931; neither of them in reality achieved the purpose intended from it, and when applied it revealed serious defects that call for comprehensive amendment. The error is not in the system itself, but in being content with it, persisting in it, and defending it — that is the whole error. People have felt the inadequacy of the present electoral law in fulfilling the purpose for which it was enacted, namely the selection of the best to represent the Ummah. Sharp criticisms have been directed at it, revealing many defects, the most important of which was pointed out by Dr. ʿAbd al-Ṣabūrī in his book *Mabādiʾ al-Qānūn al-Dustūrī* (مبادئ القانون الدستوري): that it has created an electoral body that does not truly realize the purpose of elections in the required manner; that it does not correctly express the opinion of the Ummah; and that it does not produce a body that works for the public good free from every restriction. He then supplied precise statistics from which he concluded by figures that the decisions of the Egyptian Parliament in its various sessions do not express the opinion of the Ummah, nor of its majority, nor of an active elite among its sons, but rather express the opinion of a tiny fraction of those entitled to vote, reaching in some cases no more than twelve percent. He stated that:

> The Chamber of Deputies of 1932 — despite the validity and binding force of its decisions upon the nation — represented only 8% of the electorate. The Chamber of 1921 represented 11%. The Chamber of [1936?] represented 9.52%. The Chamber of 1938 represented 5%. The Chamber of 1941 represented 17.1%. The present Chamber is no better than these.

How, after this, can it be said that this is an expression of the will of the Ummah, and that this is its genuine representation?

### Amendment and Reform

Amendment and reform of the electoral law are necessary. Among the necessary aspects of this reform are:

1. Setting special qualifications for the candidates themselves. If they represent parties, these parties must have clear programs and detailed objectives on the basis of which this candidate presents himself. If they are independent individuals, they must possess qualities and reformist methods that qualify them to represent the Ummah. This matter is closely connected with the reform of the parties in Egypt and what the political parties ought to be.

2. Setting limits to electoral propaganda and imposing penalties upon whoever violates these limits, so that it does not address families, races, or purely personal matters that have no bearing on the candidate's qualifications, but rather revolves around methods and reform plans.

3. Reforming the electoral registers and generalizing a system of verifying them; the matter of the electoral registers has become a scandal after they have been manipulated by partisan passions and governmental purposes throughout these successive periods; and making voting compulsory.

4. Imposing severe penalties for any kind of fraud and for electoral bribery.

5. If we change to election by list rather than individual election, that would be preferable and better; it would free the deputies from the pressure of their families and put public interests in place of personal interests in the estimation and conduct of the deputies.

In any case, the doors of reform and amendment are many; these are examples of them. If the resolve is sincere, the ways will become clear. The whole error lies in remaining in this state, being content with it, and turning away from the paths of reform.

## Conclusion

> "Indeed, We have sent down to you the Book in truth so that you may judge between the people by that which Allah has shown you. And do not be for the deceitful an advocate." [Qur'an, An-Nisāʾ 4:105]

I have set forth in the preceding sections the three pillars of sound government in the Islamic and parliamentary systems alike: the responsibility of the ruler, the unity of the Ummah, and respect for its will. I have pointed at length to the aspects of ambiguity in legislation, and of failure and corruption in application, in the system of government we have followed since the issuance of the Egyptian constitution until now. The result of this ambiguity, failure, and corruption is the bewilderment, weakness, and confusion in which we now find ourselves, and the division, estrangement, and scattering to which we have arrived.

There is no doubt that successive governments have been too weak to perform their duty and have lost most of their prestige in souls as a government, because of this accusation, true or false, which is thrown at them by a purely partisan spirit; and because of this incapacity resulting from the failure to define responsibility and to assume it completely. Were it not that souls in Egypt are stamped with the character of obedience and submission, and that affairs move along routine paths without renewal or initiative, everything would have come to a standstill and the confused administrative apparatus would have been unable to meet the needs of the people or to give them what they require.

### The Authority of the Law

There is no doubt that the authority of the law has been shaken and has lost most of its respect as well, because of these exceptions, favoritisms, and repeated tricks, and the occasional repeal of a law for a personal purpose — even if that repeal is by a law in outward form, the motives are always known and not hidden from anyone. This works upon souls and undermines the prestige of the law and respect for the system.

### Blind Partisanship

There is no doubt that the fire of enmity and hatred has been kindled in the souls of both rulers and ruled because of this false partisanship — which we in Egypt at one time understood as nothing more than a difference of opinion that does not spoil friendship — but which we have come to understand as hostility and hatred extending beyond consideration of public interests to boycott in all matters, public and private. When we see falsehood on the side of our opponents, and falsehood on the side of our friends, we are unable to unite our ranks in any national position on which the reform of our affairs and the future of our country depends.

This feeling of hatred, and this false understanding of partisanship that has turned into deep-rooted enmity, has diverted most intellectual and practical efforts to a matter that has consumed all the attention of our men: downfall of opponents and avoidance of their plots. The government spends most of its energy on these two concerns, and the opposition is no less occupied with them. Inevitably, this leads to the loss of rights, the wasting of interests, the rejoicing of enemies, and the benefit of the foreigner sitting upon the chest of the country.

This state of affairs has produced collapse in moral values and corruption and confusion in material affairs. The matter may reach its limit, and there will be no more room for bending the bow. A decisive, firm, reforming change is necessary. Either those in authority understand this reality and appreciate it, and hasten to make the sound reform by their own resolve and with their own hands — in which lies safety and stability, and there is still time for reform — or they persist in this neglect, and events will overtake them and the reins will slip from their hands; and none knows the outcome of that except Allah.

O people of authority in this land; O Prime Minister; O men of noble al-Azhar; O leaders of parties, bodies, and groups; O men jealous for this dear homeland:

Take hold of the matter before it is too late... before you is the ship of salvation: the system of Islam... and to Allah belong the outcomes of affairs.

I have conveyed; so witness, O Allah.
