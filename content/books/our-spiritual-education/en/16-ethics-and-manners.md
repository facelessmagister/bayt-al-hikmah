# Chapter 16: Ethics and Manners

*Adab* (أدب) is the gate whose effects reach into every topic of spiritual wayfaring to God—close and far alike. Poor manners corrupt the entire spiritual life, corrupting action, corrupting the heart, and corrupting the effects of remembrance, silence, seclusion, and solitude. It becomes impossible, with bad manners, to take from the shaykhs. Therefore, there is no spiritual wayfaring without *adab* with God and with creation. Hence they said: “By God, none has succeeded except through good manners, and none has fallen except through bad manners.”

Good manners are an expression of the refinement of the soul, of its discipline, and of control over its impulses. That alone is a sign of goodness, just as bad manners indicate that the soul remains stained by recklessness, unable to discipline itself—so it is not sound wayfaring.

*Adab* has two aspects: an outward aspect and a behavioral aspect. Knowledge usually precedes behavior and commitment. Therefore, it is necessary to define the subject of manners, while noting that the subject of manners is too broad to be encompassed in a single chapter. For there is scarcely a chapter of *fiqh* or of *tasawwuf* that does not contain issues belonging to manners. Thus, we do not hope here to mention everything; rather, we hope to mention the main points of this subject, without ignoring that there are related issues in other chapters. All of this, however, does not replace being disciplined by the Book and the Sunnah. The Book and the Sunnah are the foundation of moral and behavioral formation. The efforts of scholars derived from that do not replace study of the foundation; rather, they are precise derivations that do not dispense with the source.

Some of our shaykhs used to point out the necessity of manners with God, with human beings, with animals, and even with things. They would say: “If you treat things well, they serve you; if you do not treat them well, they do not serve you.” They gave us the example of the pitcher used for ablution: if you handle it gently in picking it up and putting it down, it serves you greatly; otherwise it will not. If this is the place of good manners with things, then what about living beings? One must interact with everything according to the correct principles of dealing, in light of God’s law. So there must be refined manners with God—gratitude, pure servitude, hope, and awe; and one must interact with His creatures in accordance with His command. The circles of manners are very wide, and we must take our share of them.

## The Need for Common Manners

It is noticeable that some environments have not yet reached common manners that become like an alphabet in daily interaction—and this has great effects on life. Other environments, however, have reached many customary manners in every aspect of life: in manner of speech, in appropriate dress for each occasion, in the way of dealing with others, in the way of advancing and holding back, and so on in the realm of general interaction. We Muslims are the most in need of the knowledge of manners of all people. And it is not merely that; our manners in every situation ought to be the most refined. But these manners are scattered here and there: in books of *fiqh*, in books of *hadith* commentary, in the various books of *tasawwuf*, and in books of *tafsir*. First and foremost, the Book and the Sunnah left no manner or noble character trait unexplained. But a comprehensive book of the Sunnah in a practical form is not found in every home, and a sound understanding of the Qur’an is not pursued by every listener. Then a thorough reading of the books of *fiqh* and *tasawwuf* is rarely achieved by anyone in its complete form. All of this has led to the contraction of the issue of manners, or its presence in limited, partial forms. Sometimes there are mistaken conceptions and dangerous behaviors that take on the appearance of manners. All of this needs treatment. The beginning of treatment is the availability of a suitable book of *tafsir*, and a comprehensive book of the Sunnah whose compilation and service require multiple conditions, as well as suitable composition in *fiqh* and *tasawwuf*.

We will mention some manners in this chapter, for the matter is broader than can be covered in a single chapter of a small book. We should note that the issue of manners in Sufi terminology is broader than in the terminology of jurists. The jurist speaks of manners in general as comprising obligations, duties, and recommended acts; but the Sufi mentions things that belong to obligations under the heading of manners, because for him *adab* is conduct and interaction with God and with His creatures. This is a point one should be aware of. In this chapter we will follow the Sufi method in mentioning some manners. Consequently, what we mention here under the title of this chapter may be an obligation, a duty, a recommended act, or even permissible. Let that be noted.

All that we will mention in this chapter is a collection of scattered sections gathered under the heading of manners and character—whether with God, with creation, or as special qualities. The Messenger of God ﷺ was a person whose character was the Qur’an. So what we mention here, and what others mention, is only a reminder of some matters; no one aspires to comprehensiveness.

Reaching the refinement of the soul, which is the goal, is difficult or incomplete or impossible without these conditions. Therefore, the matter requires knowledge of the conditions, and every condition requires manners. There is no character trait that is separate from a corresponding manner. We will not reach refinement unless this is accompanied by manners. Humility as a quality of the soul needs an outward expression, which is manners. Forbearance as a quality of the soul needs an outward expression, which is manners. Respecting and honoring people as a quality of the soul needs an outward expression, which is manners. The sounder the wayfaring, the more the conditions of wayfaring are realized, and the more manners are present, the more certain is the arrival at refinement. And the more refinement is achieved, the greater one’s ability to refine those whom God has placed in one’s care.

Thus, the issue of manners and character is a vast one. One of the first characteristics of the Sufi is to pursue noble character traits and good manners and to realize them. Hence they said: “Tasawwuf is character. So whoever surpasses you in character has surpassed you in tasawwuf.”

Once these meanings are clear, let us begin presenting some sections.

## Section: On the Subject of Ethics and Manners

The author of *al-Mabahith al-Asliyah* composed a passage on the subject of character and manners on the path; we quote it here, with brief comments on some words in it.

He said:

> “The path has an outward and an inward.”

That is, the path to God has an outward and an inward. He will explain this in two lines; briefly, its outward is what relates to reforming the outward limbs, and its inward is what relates to reforming the inner states. “You know from it the health of the inward.” That is, from the outward of the path you know the soundness of the inward of the wayfarers. He informs us that the uprightness of the outward is an indication from which one knows the uprightness of the inward. He expressed uprightness as health, for the health of the outward is a sign of the health of the inward.

He then explained the outward of the path by saying:

> “Its outward is manners and character, with every creature that has a due.”

*Al-khuluq* here means one’s share or due. Thus the outward of the path is good manners with every creature of God, even with one who has no share of manners, let alone anything else. *Adab* is the best stance in every situation one encounters, in accordance with God’s law. There are situations in which the mannerly response is anger, and situations in which the most refined manner is doing good, restraining anger, and so on. It is a subtle meaning that only one granted success understands. Only one who is truly wise knows how to place everything in its proper place. It was among the character traits of the Messenger of God ﷺ that he would not become angry on his own account, but if the sanctities of God were violated, nothing could stop his anger; and if he saw an evil, his displeasure would not subside until that evil ceased.

Then he explained the inward of the path by saying:

> “Its inward is the stations of spiritual states, with the stations of the Majestic One.”

The divine influx, when it descends into the heart, produces an effect; this effect is called a *hal* (spiritual state). The stations of states are the hearts; but in the line he meant the good spiritual states themselves, because he mentioned *maqamat* (stations) after that in association with them. He said: “with the stations of the Majestic One.” The difference between a *hal* and a *maqam* is that a *hal* changes: it comes and goes, unlike a *maqam*, which is firm and stable. So the inward of the path consists of states and stations in wayfaring to God, the Lord of the Worlds. It is as if he said: the inward of the wayfarer to God is a constant transition from state to station, and from station to station. All of this is the inward of the path.

Then the author began to speak about *adab*:

> “And good manners, outwardly visible, are an indication of a person’s inward.”

This is part of what preceded: the soundness of the outward indicates the soundness of the inward. “They are also a support for the poor.” That is, the poor person relies on them to rise to higher stations in religion and worldly life, because hearts are naturally inclined to love people of good manners. “And for the rich, an adornment and honor.” Thus good manners adorn the rich, ennoble them, and raise their standing. In this line, he means that neither rich nor poor can dispense with good manners.

> “And it is said: whoever is denied the dominion of manners, he is far, however near he draws.”

*Al-tadanni* and *al-qurb* mean the same thing. The meaning is: whoever has no manners is far from God and from His creatures, however close he imagines himself to be. Abu Hafs said: “Tasawwuf is entirely *adab*: every time has its *adab*, every state has its *adab*, every station has its *adab*. Whoever adheres to the manners of times reaches the rank of men. Whoever neglects manners is far from where he imagines closeness, and rejected from where he imagines acceptance.”

> “How many are held back by lineage, yet manners set them free.”

Some said: “One whom lineage prevents from rising in ranks, pleasing manners release to the highest ranks.”

After clarifying the place of manners in life in general, he returns to *tasawwuf* and says:

> “So by manners the people truly prevailed... From it the people gained what they gained.”

Here “the people” means the Sufis. That is, the Sufis attained eminence and honor only through manners, and they gained knowledge, gnosis, lights, secrets, outward and inward miracles only through manners. Then he mentioned some of their manners:

> “When they advised the youths and young ones.”

*Al-ahdath*: plural of *hadith*, one who has not yet grown a beard. *Al-asaghir*: plural of *small*, here meaning those below the age of *hadith*. He points out that one of the most important manners of the Sufis was their sincere advice to young people and adolescents. I say: while noting the caution of the Sufis about companionship with adolescents and their fear for their hearts.

> “And served the shaykhs and brothers.”

*Al-sadat* here means the devotees, ascetics, righteous, practicing scholars, and wayfaring disciples who have not reached the rank of shaykhhood. *Al-akabir* here means the shaykhs. Serving *sadat* and *akabir* is done through reverence, modesty, and giving each rank its due in every respect.

Then he mentioned their manners in speech:

> “And they avoided what pains hearts.”

This is their manner with every Muslim: they would not speak to a Muslim in a way that hurt his heart, even if it were advice. Exhortation only benefits when done with gentleness and wisdom. This is especially important in avoiding what hurts with one’s wife, family, and brothers.

Then he mentioned their manners in action:

> “And they hastened to the obligatory and the recommended.”

This alludes to the perfection of their servitude: they hasten to fulfill the rights of their Lord, whether obligatory or recommended.

Then he mentioned their manners with shaykhs and brothers:

> “And they served shaykhs and brothers.”

Service to people of God is of great importance in the foundations of wayfaring to God, because of what it produces in the souls of those who perform it: humility and deep realization of the meaning of being lowly before the believers—which is a root of character in Islam. Whoever is not accustomed to serving his brothers has a thick veil between himself and lowliness before the believers. There is no doubt that serving the shaykh has an additional virtue, because of the reverence for elders in both knowledge and age. People sometimes reject or look down upon such service, but this rejection is misplaced. Ibn Mas’ud used to serve the Messenger of God ﷺ, and Anas ibn Malik was dedicated to serving him. Disdaining service to brothers and shaykhs is connected to arrogance, haughtiness, and other diseases that one must strive against in oneself.

> “And they devoted their souls and bodies.”

That is, in this service to shaykhs and brothers.

Then he mentioned their manners in knowledge and remembrance:

> “And they gave precedence in the gatherings of remembrance.”

That is, each of them would give his brother a turn during the sessions of remembrance, until he finished speaking. If any speech occurred, there would be no shouting, no quarreling, and no departure from manners.

> “And they respected the one who preceded and the one who came after.”

The “one who preceded” refers to the Companions, the Followers, the saints, the righteous, the practicing scholars, and the scholars of the *umma* who exercised *ijtihad*. Respecting them means not mentioning them except with goodness, and being careful about the weight of one’s words concerning them. The “one who came after” means respecting people of one’s own time, even if they came later or are younger in age. They would not look down upon later generations with contempt; rather, they knew that the bounty of God has no limit.

> “And they asked the shaykh about what they did not know.”

Because seeking knowledge is obligatory upon every Muslim, and is known from the religion by necessity. It extends to knowledge of one’s immediate situation—whether action, state, or station.

> “And they paused at what they had not reached.”

That is, they would not speak about a station they had not reached with the talk of a claimant, nor would they ask except about what was appropriate to their state, avoiding pretension. Or, they would not speak except about knowledge; if their knowledge did not reach something, they would pause. They would refrain from discussing what they had not attained knowledge of.

> “And they elevated themselves through whatever knowledge they possessed.”

Their knowledge was great, and their action corresponded to their knowledge; for action is the fruit of knowledge. Knowledge without action is a means without an end. Among their sayings: “Knowledge calls out to action: if it finds it, it stays; otherwise, it departs.” Whoever acts without knowledge, God leaves him to the evil consequences of that ignorance.

> “And they preferred, pardoned, and were modest.”

These are three character traits of theirs in knowledge and elsewhere. They preferred others over themselves in speech, in seating arrangements and gatherings, and in every matter of honor—unless someone else gave them precedence, let alone preferring others in food, wealth, position, and the like. They were modest: they shunned indecent or unrefined words in remembrance or elsewhere, whether aroused by others or through a lapse in manners, let alone their own avoidance of indecent conduct or blameworthy speech. “*Al-Ightifar*” here means pardoning and overlooking the harshness of brothers who are still in the process of training, and being patient with roughness in remembrance and otherwise.

> “And they rubbed against justice and fairness.”

That is, they accustomed themselves to justice and fairness, and if they judged, they judged with justice and fairness. They judged with justice among themselves and upon themselves. If any right was due to someone, they would render it justly and submit to the truth, without fanaticism and without being provoked by pre-Islamic tribalism. *Al-insaf* is acknowledging the truth whenever it appears, without hesitation. They used to say: “Fairness is the scent of nobility.”

> “And they drank from every pure flowing spring.”

A flowing spring is running water that does not cease; “pure” means unmixed. The meaning is that because the Sufis judged with justice and were characterized by fairness, they drank from the purest and clearest of knowledges.

> “And some of them were a help to others.”

Realizing His saying:

> “And cooperate in righteousness and piety...” [Quran, Al-Ma’idah 5:2]

A Muslim helps his Muslim brother with his self, his wealth, his influence, his knowledge, his state, his spiritual station, his sincere advice, and his affection, and so on.

> “He finds with him ease and safety.”

*Al-du’a*: rest; *al-amn*: security. That is, each of them finds with his brother rest for his soul and safety for his self, his honor, his trust, and his aims.

> “He helps him in piety wherever they may be.”

Realizing his saying ﷺ: “Help your brother, whether he is oppressed or an oppressor.” It was said: “O Messenger of God, I understand helping the oppressed, but how do I help the oppressor?” He said: “You prevent him by your hand from his oppression.”

> “And if he wronged him, he answered with goodness.”

That is, if a Sufi wronged his brother in word or deed, he would pardon him and treat him with goodness in return for the wrong. He would repay evil with goodness, realizing His saying:

> “Repel evil by that which is better...” [Quran, Fussilat 41:34]

Then after some lines in which he discusses a matter that people think is manners but is not, he says:

> “And the aim of this path is manners... In every state of it, this is the doctrine.”

In this line, he indicates that the path is built upon manners—indeed, manners are the crux of the path. So whoever has no manners has no path. With the last line, the passage that the author of *al-Mabahith al-Asliyah* composed on character and manners comes to an end. We have quoted some comments on it, drawing on Ibn ‘Ajibah’s commentary on this poem as our source material.

In connection with the first line of this passage, Ibn ‘Ajibah said: “The inward of the path is the place where the states and stations descend, and that is the hearts and secrets, for there is nothing hidden except God. The difference between a *hal* and a *maqam* is that a *hal* changes, comes and goes, unlike a *maqam*, which is firm and stable.”

In *al-‘Awarif*, it is said: “There is much confusion between *hal* and *maqam*, and the allusions of the shaykhs differ in this regard because of the confusion: they resemble one another in themselves and intermingle. So something may appear to some as a *hal*, and to others as a *maqam*. Both views are valid because of the intermingling. But a criterion must be mentioned to distinguish between them, though the wording and expression carry the difference. A *hal* is called *hal* because of its change; a *maqam* is called *maqam* because of its stability and settledness. Something may be a *hal* and then become a *maqam*. For example, an urge to take oneself to account may arise from the servant’s inward, then disappear due to the dominance of egoistic traits, then return, then disappear. The servant is repeatedly visited by this state of self-accounting. If divine assistance comes and the state of self-accounting prevails, the soul is subdued and disciplined, and self-accounting becomes its abode and settled station. Then the state of vigilance descends upon it. If self-accounting has become its station, vigilance becomes a state for it. The state of vigilance then changes, alternating between heedlessness and inattention in the servant’s inward, until the fog of heedlessness clears and God grants him assistance. Then the station of vigilance becomes a station after having been a state. The station of self-accounting is not truly stable unless visited by the state of vigilance, and the station of vigilance is not truly stable unless visited by the state of contemplation. If the servant is granted the descent of the state of contemplation, his vigilance becomes stable and turns into his station. The state of contemplation is also a state: it is concealed by veiling and appears by unveiling, then becomes a station, and its sun is freed from the eclipse of concealment. Within the station of contemplation there are states, increases, and elevations from one state to a higher one, such as realization through *fana’*, transition to *baqa’*, and ascent from the eye of certainty to the truth of certainty—a certainty that pierces the pericardium of the heart. That is the highest branch of contemplation.”

Likewise, repentance, godliness, asceticism, reliance, contentment, and surrender are states and then become stations. As long as they are struggled for, they are states; when they become tasted, they are stations. They said: “States are gifts, for a gift from God is a reward for works; stations are acquisitions, for acquisition from them is earned through persistence in works.” In reality, all are gifts.

## The Manners of the Limbs

The Sufi said: “For every limb there is a special manner.” God said:

> “Indeed, the hearing, the sight, and the heart—each of those will be questioned.” [Quran, Al-Isra’ 17:36]

One of the shaykhs said: “Good manners with God is that no limb of yours moves except in what pleases God.”

### Manners of the Tongue

The tongue should be moist with the remembrance of God, with mentioning brothers well, with praying for them, with giving sincere advice and exhortation. It should not speak of what they dislike, nor backbite, nor slander—slander meaning walking with ill intent—nor insult, nor engage in what does not concern it. If one is in a gathering, one should speak with them about what concerns them; if they move to what does not concern them, one should leave them and remain silent. One should speak in every place what befits the situation. It was said: “For every station there is speech.” And: “God created the tongue as a translator of the heart and a key to good and evil.” And: “If you seek the soundness of your heart, seek help in guarding your tongue, and adhere to silence, for it conceals the ignorant and adorns the wise.” A Companion said: “Will people not be thrown into the Fire upon their faces except because of what their tongues reap?”

The manners of the ear are that it should not hear foulness, obscenity, backbiting, slander, or evil things. It was said:

> A noble youth is known by his refusal of evil,
> As though shielded from every vile thing.
> Rather, he hears remembrance, exhortation, and wisdom,
> And what benefits him in religion and worldly life,
> And he listens attentively.

### Manners of the Eye

The eye should lower its gaze from the forbidden, from the faults of brothers, from indecencies, and from forbidden things. God knows the treachery of the eyes and what the hearts conceal. It was said: “Whoever obeys his glance is hastening his destruction,” and in another narration: “Whoever lets his glance roam will die an accidental death.” Indeed, whatever you send your glance ahead as a scout for your heart, one day the sights will tire you with what you are not capable of bearing, nor patient toward. The Sufi said: “Whoever lowers his gaze perfects his chivalry,” and: “Whoever has many glances has lasting regrets.” One’s gaze should be with reflection and inference upon the power and greatness of God and the perfection of His making, free from the appetites of the lower self.

### Manners of the Heart

The heart should observe the praiseworthy spiritual states, reflect upon the blessings and bounties of God, and upon the wonders of His creation. God said:

> “Those who remember Allah while standing, sitting, and lying on their sides, and reflect upon the creation of the heavens and the earth, saying: Our Lord, You did not create this in vain...” [Quran, Aal ‘Imran 3:191]

Among the manners of the heart are having good thoughts of God and of all Muslims, and purifying it from suspicion, envy, betrayal, bad thoughts, and evil assumptions. Betrayal comes from the eye: God said:

> “Indeed, the hearing, the sight, and the heart—each of those will be questioned.” [Quran, Al-Isra’ 17:36]

The Prophet ﷺ said: “Indeed, in the body there is a morsel: if it is sound, the whole body is sound; and if it is corrupt, the whole body is corrupt. Indeed, it is the heart.”

Al-Sari al-Saqti said: “Hearts are of three kinds: a heart like a mountain—nothing moves it; a heart like a palm tree—its root is firm, but the wind bends it this way and that; and a heart like a feather—it goes with every wind and does not remain still.”

### Manners of the Hands and Feet

The hands should be extended in goodness and kindness, serve the brothers, and not be used to help in disobeying God. The feet should walk in what benefits one’s soul and one’s brothers; one should not walk in jest, in treachery, with strutting or arrogance, for God dislikes that. They should not be used to help in sin.

## The Meaning of Character

As for *al-akhlaq* (character traits), they mean good character with every creature. Their source is forbearance, pardon, and patience—or, as one may say, their source is that you treat creation as you would like to be treated. Or one may say: their source is withholding harm, offering sacrifice, fairness in outward and inward matters, bearing coarseness while witnessing purity, turning one’s back to the world.

Al-Ghazali said: “It is mastery of the soul at the moment of desire and anger.” It returns to what was mentioned.

## The Rank of Manners

In connection with the author’s words “So by manners the people truly prevailed...,” Ibn ‘Ajibah said: “*Al-su’dad* means honor. That is, the people attained eminence and honor only through manners with God, with His Messenger, with their shaykhs, and with all Muslims. Manners with God are: carrying out His command, avoiding His prohibition, surrendering to His overwhelming power.”

Shaykh Zarruq, may God be pleased with him, said in commenting on this: “It is preserving limits, fulfilling covenants, clinging to the Loving God, contentment with what exists, and exerting effort.”

Manners with the Messenger of God ﷺ are: following his Sunnah, preferring his beloved ones, being guided by his guidance, and being characterized by his character. Manners with shaykhs are, in addition to what has preceded: reverence, respectful humility, service, keeping their company, and doing for them what you would like for yourself or more.

The manners of the limbs are necessary, as are the manners of times: filling them with acts of obedience. Times of the servant are four, as Shaykh Abu al-‘Abbas, may God have mercy on him, said: a time of obedience, a time of sin, a time of blessing, and a time of affliction. The time of obedience requires of you witnessing the grace; the time of sin requires realizing repentance; the time of blessing requires gratitude; the time of affliction requires patience. If the servant upholds all these manners, complete honor and great closeness with the elite and the common people are his.

## Sincere Advice to Young People

In connection with the author’s words “When they advised the youths and young ones,” Ibn ‘Ajibah said: “They advised them by planting goodness in their hearts.” Ibn Abi Zayd said in his epistle: “The hearts most receptive to goodness are those that evil reaches first.” The Sufi, may God have mercy on him, said: “Companionship with young ones is through compassion, guidance, discipline, and accustoming them to what the school of thought requires, continuing with what benefits them, not with what gratifies them; with what profits them, not with what they love; and restraining them from what does not concern them.”

## Avoiding What Pains Hearts

In connection with the author’s words “And they avoided what pains hearts,” Ibn ‘Ajibah said—may God have mercy on al-Shafi‘i, who said:

> If you wish to live, and your religion to remain sound,
> And your honor to be preserved and your reputation protected,
> Then guard the faults of people as you would your own,
> And if your eye reveals to you faults, lower it and say:
> “O eye, for people there are eyes.”
> And accompany with kindness, and avoid one who transgresses,
> But that which is better...

Shaykh Zarruq said: “These lines comprehensively gather all that pains hearts through avoidance. Whoever acts upon them is healed from these ailments, whose root is all spying into people’s affairs and having bad thoughts of them.”

We conclude this section with a saying of Ibn ‘Ata’ on *adab*, and one of al-Junayd.

Ibn ‘Ata’ said: “Beware of the existence of His goodness to you and the persistence of your bad conduct toward Him, lest that be a gradual drawing-on of punishment. We will gradually draw them from where they do not know.” That is, let not the servant, ignorant of his situation, behave badly in manners so that punishment is delayed from him. Let him say: “If this were bad manners, the divine support would be cut off and remoteness would be required.” One may have the support cut off from him without realizing it—and if nothing else, he is prevented from increase. One may be placed in a state of remoteness without knowing it—and if nothing else, he is left to what he wants.

Al-Junayd said: “We did not take tasawwuf from speech and talk, argumentation and debate; rather, we took it from hunger, staying awake at night, and abundant works.”

## Section: On Some Manners of the Shaykhs

Among the manners of shaykhs, mentors, and callers is that they begin with disciples and students through gentleness, kindness, and compassion, so that they may reach their hearts, discover their readiness, and remove the veils between them. Often, a mentor or shaykh or caller overwhelms disciples and students with burdens, causing them to become averse or flee. Such beginnings are flawed, because they oppose wisdom.

There is a difference between a disciple who comes to a shaykh and asks him to teach him a book—such a person may well be begun with knowledge directly—and a person who comes seeking guidance or information. In such a case, it is better to begin with acquaintance, question and answer, light pleasantries, and then a non-burdensome assignment. In this regard, al-Junayd said: “When you meet a poor seeker, do not begin with knowledge; begin with gentleness, for knowledge estranges him, while gentleness brings him close.”

Imam al-Ghazali commented on this: “Through the gentleness of the Sufis toward those who emulate them, the beginner benefits. The more a person among them had of state and action, the more gentle he was with the beginner.”

Among the manners of shaykhs, mentors, and callers is that they try to move a person, even a small step, toward goodness. Every movement toward goodness, however small, pushes a person toward God, for it is among the attributes of God that whoever draws near to Him by a handspan, He draws near to him by an armspan. Therefore, any shifting of a person from one state to a higher state, with a sincere intention, propels him toward the door of God. Those engaged in calling and training must exert effort to move a person one step, however simple, because that step may be a prelude to another that is higher and more refined.

Among the manners of shaykhs is much listening to every speaker, knowing what is true and what is not, and distinguishing between one who speaks truth and one who does not; then knowing the limits of agreement with others. All of this we take from His saying:

> “And among them are those who harm the Prophet and say, ‘He is all ear.’ Say: ‘An ear of goodness for you...’” [Quran, Al-Tawbah 9:61]

And from His saying:

> “And know that among you is the Messenger of God. If he were to obey you in much of the matter, you would be in difficulty...” [Quran, Al-Hujurat 49:7]

So whatever involves hardship for people and burdening of their resolves, the Messenger of God ﷺ would not obey anyone in it. Among his manners ﷺ is that he “whenever given a choice between two matters, would choose the easier of them, unless it was a sin.”

The principal manner of the shaykh, after learning, is the purification of souls. Purification revolves between emptying and adorning. In the matter of emptying and adorning, you are between two approaches: either you adorn yourself with a character trait and empty yourself of another, then move to another until you reach the peak in refinement; or you strike one blow at the root of every blameworthy character trait, realize the source from which every noble character trait springs, and then everything else comes. This is another path. They say: Alexander the Great, before beginning the conquest of Persia, passed by a temple, and its priests said to him: “No one conquers Persia except one who can untie this knot of intertwined cords.” Alexander had only to strike the bundle with his sword, and all its knots came undone. Likewise, the perfected shaykh, when a sincere disciple comes to him, can with one blow untie all his knots so that he sets out anew.

The shortest way for the soul to realize every refinement and to be emptied of every deficiency is to place it in a situation in which it is released at once from its self-sovereignty and adorned with servanthood, beautified with the attributes of refinement. The greatest shaykh is the one who knows how to place the disciple at this starting point. The truest seekers are those who do not mind doing what they are commanded in order to reach this.

Let us explain: the lowest, base character traits are arrogance, self-conceit, and self-approval; from these spring every vice. If there is anything of this in the heart, it is veiled from the Truth, from acceptance, from benefit, and from God and His signs. Unless the heart is freed from these diseases, no good can be hoped from it; rather, it is likely to harbor envy, malice, aggression, rancor, oppression, turning away from the path of God, and the like. It is enough to indicate this that God said:

> “I will turn away from My signs those who are arrogant upon the earth without right...” [Quran, Al-A‘raf 7:146]

The shortest path, then, is for a person to empty himself of all these meanings at once. The beginning of that is readiness to receive. If one accepts being a student and places himself under training, he immediately leaves behind a large part of these meanings. If the mentor knows God, knows the law, and is expert in the diseases of souls, he may advise him concerning something, or require it of him, freeing him from the remaining traces of these meanings—such as commanding him to serve his brothers, or to humble himself before God and sit wherever the gathering ends, or to be a student to one beneath him, or to act contrary to his lower self. If the seeker of God does such things, he is freed from every shackle, and he no longer sees anything but the Creator, launching forth with a new heart.

This is the shaykh’s first task; then his other tasks follow. But this will not be accomplished unless sincerity is found in the disciple. Most people do not combine knowledge, feeling, and action; or knowledge and state. They do not know the way to complete these. This is a matter of immense ignorance.

There is knowledge of God and His law; sometimes you may find knowledge of God and His law without God-consciousness, and sometimes God-consciousness without refinement in character. What is the secret? The secret is that knowledge of God does not pass from its rational, inferential frame to its tasted, felt frame. If it does not move to the frame of taste and feeling, it does not give complete direction. Sometimes the failure of mentors is that they do not know the way to transfer a person from inferential knowledge of God to felt knowledge of Him, the Mighty and Majestic. Therefore, a large gap remains between knowledge and feeling.

God said:

> “...And know that God intervenes between a person and his heart...” [Quran, Al-Anfal 8:24]

And sometimes the cause is not the mentor’s incapacity, but people’s lack of zeal for such things, due to not knowing the true value of things. If one knew that increase in knowledge of God is purchased with souls, such a person would be close to attaining it. As for one who does not know this, how can he exert his effort or work hard in that?

To know that God is Hearing, Seeing, and Powerful is the most obligatory of obligations upon you; but to see all your actions as the action of God is a sound fruit of the first knowledge.

The problem with many people is that their heart-feelings stop at one limit and do not surpass it. The task of the shaykh is to transfer a person in the realm of feelings from one stage to the next, and not to leave him at lower feelings when higher ones exist. This is the path of sound training, and it is the path to fulfilling the condition of action. As for the spiritual feelings: surrender to God means carrying out His command. The more you know that everything is His doing, the more trust in Him is realized. The more you know that everything besides Him is transient, the more sincerity to Him is realized. The more you know His majesty, the more one fears disobeying Him. The more you know His generosity, the more one obeys Him. These are some of the tasks of the shaykhs. If the shaykh fails in this, he has missed the most important matters.

The shaykh’s first two tasks are teaching and purification. This requires effort, arrangement, and organization of many matters. For wayfaring requires diligent study, the wisdom of the mentor, and the going through by the student of periods of languor, periods of activity, periods of spiritual aridity, and periods of the dominance of desire. Therefore, attending the general gathering was necessary, so that the wayfarer’s spirit might take from the spirits of his brothers, and his heart might be strengthened by the spirits of his brothers, and so that he might hear what stirs ambition and divine concern in his heart. The gathering has a special blessing, a special tranquility, and special manifestations.

If all this is clear, is there a specific remembrance and a specific recitation to reach these meanings? What the Messenger of God ﷺ and his Companions were upon, and what the Sunnah supports, and what the state of the *umma* testifies to, is that there is no single specified remembrance. The Messenger of God ﷺ did not teach every Companion a specific *wird*. And the Sufi orders each have their own *wird*, yet they say the end is one. So the matter returns to wisdom—the wisdom of the mentor, and the readiness and state of the student. For every remembrance has its effects on the soul, and souls differ. The important thing is that the mentor knows the effect of every remembrance on a person’s soul, and gives each person what suits his state, and directs his attention to what should be observed.

If he instructs him in *La ilaha illa Allah*, for example, he directs his attention to one meaning of *La ilaha illa Allah* one time, and to another meaning another time; or he orders him to observe the meanings one by one in a single session. If he instructs him in the remembrance of the Name Allah, he orders him to behold all outward existence as this Name, then to behold all hidden existence as this Name, again and again. All of this is among the first tasks of the shaykh.

But alongside and despite all this, he must make the student feel that he is part of an *umma*, and train him to be able to exist within the single Islamic rank; and that both he and the student are in this rank, wayfaring on the path to realize Islamic objectives at every level, bearing what that requires of sacrifice and hardship. All of this is the shaykh’s manner, indeed his duty.

In return, the disciple must realize sincerity in seeking, and acquire good manners. The first of these is complete respect that is not diminished by speaking the truth, or by the pure sincere advice he offers the shaykh. We are an *umma* whose general manner is the respect of the young for the old and the compassion of the old for the young, within the framework of pure sincere advice among all, and broad consultation, which is the manner of the community—while noting that every issue has its own circle of consultation according to its nature.

## Section: On the General Morality of the Sufi

The author of *al-Mabahith* said: “They attributed the Sufi to refinement,” because of his inheritance of knowledge, action, and state, and his taking refinement from the stations of Islam, faith, excellence, God-consciousness, and gratitude. He took from the station of Islam the highest degrees of action; from the station of faith certainty and tranquility; from the station of excellence vigilance and contemplation; from the station of God-consciousness the refinement of uprightness upon God’s command; and from the station of gratitude pure servitude outwardly and inwardly. “And they struck similitudes of him in likenesses.” They set forth for the Sufi parables by which they expressed his attainment of this refinement, and these will now follow.

> “He is like the breeze in loftiness.”

That is, the Sufi is like the breeze in gentleness, in people’s need of him, and in their hardly sensing his presence. His actions are extremely gentle and simple, and people are greatly in need of them. They scarcely feel him except when he is gone, because of his abundant gentleness, his lack of affectation, and the harmony of his action with reason, innate disposition, and behavior close to the soul. Then he is also like the breeze in that he is above the earth while still mingling with it: he is with his fellow human beings, but in the loftiness of servitude to God he is distinct from others, elevated above them without being haughty. Great is the difference between the two states.

> “Then like the earth in nearness.”

He is like the earth toward people: they walk upon it, and it bears them, and gives them of its abundant fruits; indeed, every foul thing is thrown upon it, and it gives good. So the Sufi is in the utmost humility, forbearance, endurance, and giving.

> “Then like fire in brightness.”

That is, he is like fire in that it illuminates from one side, and from another side burns whatever is cast into it. So the Sufi lights the path for people, and burns every vile character trait in himself; and through his speech, example, and direction, he burns the vile character traits of everyone who mixes with him, accompanies him, or studies under him.

> “Then like water in quenching.”

The Sufi quenches the hearts thirsty for good and in need of irrigation, with faith and certainty; and he quenches the spirits thirsty for knowledge of God and servitude to Him; and he quenches the minds thirsty for pure realities. So the perfected Sufi—this is his affair in his gentleness, humility, and illumination of the path.

## Section: On the Method of Wisdom in Calling to God

Some of our shaykhs held that in our age we should observe something important in calling to God in order to bring back those whose Islamic roots have been shaken to their Islam. There are many cases in which you meet a Muslim by origin whose knots have been tied by many things, to the point that disbelief almost stole him—or did steal him—so that nothing remains of Islam for him but the name. And often you do not find an opportunity to say anything to this person. Since we are now in a position of weakness, the shaykh would advise us to use the weapon of *ihsan*. It is *ihsan* that extracts the good from a person’s heart, if there is good in it. From *ihsan* come forbearance and patience. It was among the character of our Messenger ﷺ that the intensity of ignorance against him only increased him in gentleness. Through *ihsan* we can reach some hearts; through it we can say a word or lessen a resentment, and all of that becomes a means of guidance.

Sincerity is necessary in this and in every matter, as is observing the manner of the time, the right of the time, and the duty of the time; then asking God to place us in the appropriate stance for every situation. Our scholars mentioned that calling to God begins with clarification, then exhortation, then rebuke, then threat. This advice is suitable as a prelude to clarification in some cases, and when the right of the law requires it. But sometimes the right of the law in some situations may require censure, rebuke, or other things. All of that should be observed. Only one granted wisdom can place things in their proper places, and there is no wisdom except by the enabling of God.

## Section: On a Great Character Trait the Sufis Emphasize

Among the well-known Sufi expressions is: “The Sufis are at their best when they rebuke one another.” This expression, one of the most famous inherited in Sufi circles, means that the Sufis are at their best when some of them command others with good and forbid others from evil; that is, they are at their best when none of them remains silent about the evil of his brother. The truth is that Muslims generally do not succeed unless this happens, for God said:

> “By time, indeed, mankind is in loss, except for those who have believed and done righteous deeds and advised each other to truth and advised each other to patience.” [Quran, Al-‘Asr 103:1-3]

There is no success for a person unless faith, righteous deed, mutual advising to truth, and mutual advising to patience are combined. Mutual advising to truth and patience is one of the pillars of salvation with God. The Jews deserved the curse of God by abandoning commanding good and forbidding evil among themselves. The Messenger of God ﷺ warned us of the curse of God and the scattering of our hearts if we do not command one another with good and forbid one another from evil. It is among the ways of God that He does not unite the hearts of His servants unless commanding good and forbidding evil are among their character traits. God said:

> “The believing men and believing women are allies of one another. They enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong and establish prayer and give zakah and obey Allah and His Messenger. Those—Allah will have mercy upon them...” [Quran, Al-Tawbah 9:71]

If these qualities are gathered in them, they are the ones promised by God mercy, one of whose effects is the unity of hearts upon God. God said:

> “And no different parties will there be, except those upon whom your Lord has bestowed mercy...” [Quran, Hud 11:118-119]

Those who are mercifully treated are those who do not differ. There are no recipients of this special mercy except those in whom a group of character traits is gathered, among the most important of which are commanding good and forbidding evil. How far is this from the state of people today! This is a manifestation of deficiency. And in some circles, matters are often the opposite of what they should be: instead of training a person to command good, forbid evil, and give sincere advice, he is trained to submit to the state of the shaykh, even if he sees him upon evil; and instead of making the disciple know all good and all evil through sound knowledge, ignorance of good and evil becomes widespread and overwhelming, to the point that good becomes evil and evil becomes good. How difficult that is, and how far from the guidance of God’s religion! Therefore, a complete return to this character trait is necessary, until it takes on the character of instinct in every Muslim’s thought and behavior. Each of us should simply say to his brother: “This is wrong, my brother,” and the other say: “May God reward you with good, my brother”—said with all the manners of the young toward the old, and with all humility by which the older accepts it even from the tongue of the younger.

As for the shaykh, he should encourage this and be glad, so that the disciples become accustomed to it. Everyone must take a firm stance against evil until it ceases, while observing that evil should be removed in the wise manner that does not lead to a greater evil, and without exceeding the legal limits in rebuke. Imam al-Ghazali, the proof of Islam, in his *Ihya’ ‘Ulum al-Din* discussed evil in a manner I do not think one finds its like in its chapter; let the reader consult it.

## Section: On Some of Their Manners Regarding Food

From the words of the author of *al-Mabahith al-Asliyah* on this subject:

> “And the people’s manners at food: leaving concern for it.”

That is, the manners of the Sufis in eating or before eating are many: among them is not being preoccupied with food before needing it, unless one has responsibility for it on behalf of others. “And infrequently mentioning it if absent.” That is, among their manners is rarely mentioning food before it arrives, because mentioning it is a sign of the soul’s attachment and longing for it. “Because for them it is a veil.” That is, because mentioning it is a veil from many things, through the soul’s preoccupation with it, due to its eager seeking and remembering. For a person to mention it much is distraction and waste of much time in unimportant matters, apart from being a sign of weakness of servitude and lack of true attachment to God. “They treated it like medicine.” That is, the Sufis placed food and drink in the position of medicine for maintaining this body, so they would partake of it only to the extent of its healing benefit, which is what preserves it—taking from the sound *hadith*: “The son of Adam does not fill any vessel worse than his stomach.” So they would not take from it more than what maintains the body, nor mention it or think about it much, busying themselves with what is more important: remembrance, thought, contemplation, or outward interaction. And when they ate, they intended by it strength for obedience to God.

> “They were not concerned with gathering it, earning it, its surplus, or withholding it.”

Since the wayfarer to God seeks to reach God and His pleasure, among his manners is that in every action he performs, his action should be entirely obedience to God and execution of His command. So if securing food becomes for them, or for their dependents, an obligation, a duty, or a recommended act, then they do it while observing that. Ibn ‘Ajibah said: “Whoever among them busied himself with any of the means, that was for him upholding the custom of servitude; and if anything came from it, they were trustees in it, as if they were treasurers of the kingdom: they hold what they were commanded to hold and send forth what they were commanded to send forth.” “Surplus” in the line means extra food; they are not concerned with its surplus, nor do they withhold food from God’s creatures out of miserliness, but rather in the way we mentioned. “They did not belittle it nor find fault with it.” That is, among the manners of the people when food arrived was not to belittle it by disdaining it; and among their manners was not to find fault with food, in accordance with the Sunnah of the Messenger of God ﷺ, in the *hadith*: “The Messenger of God never found fault with food; if he desired it, he ate it, otherwise he left it.” So they did not despise food, even if it was little or poor. Among their manners was to receive the little from its owner who brought it with generosity, joy, esteem, making much of it, and blessing; and to begin eating it before others, to please the owner’s heart and be gentle with his feelings. They did likewise with coarse or poor food.

> “It was not an end in itself, so they would seek it.”

That is, food for the Sufis was not an end in itself; they did not seek it in a way that made it a goal in itself, as is the case with the greedy and the gluttonous. “And the people did not store food.” This is the height of manners regarding food and other things. God said:

> “They ask you what they should spend. Say: ‘The surplus...’” [Quran, Al-Baqarah 2:219]

The early Sufis would take only what they needed at the time and give the surplus in charity. The later Sufis differed in their *ijtihad* after the spread of unlawful wealth, people’s stinginess, and the paralysis of rulings in the Islamic community, until some of them considered the shaykh’s independence from his disciples to be among his character traits. That is only possible if he has wealth. In origin, they did not condemn saving; the Messenger of God ﷺ used to store a year’s provision for his family in the latter part of his life. So this matter has various states, and the legal ruling depends on time, place, and person. “Rather, they left the unlawful out of God-consciousness, and they left expansion in the lawful out of godliness.” Ibn ‘Ajibah said: “They left the lawful out of asceticism, they left the unlawful out of God-consciousness, and they left the doubtful out of godliness.” “Except a little of what is easily available.” That is, except a little of the lawful that is readily available. What drove them to minimize even the lawful was the difficulty of obtaining pure lawful wealth due to the corruption of transactions, the weakness of knowledge of the lawful and unlawful among most people, and the scarcity of godliness. Hence the author of the poem said:

> “Since pure lawful wealth has become hard to find.”

*Al-halal al-mahd* is what is pure, unmixed, and without disagreement, or what is lawful in relation to the knowledge of God—what He has not burdened us with, nor with much corruption. This type of pure lawful wealth has become scarce, so the Sufis bound themselves to eat within the limits of need in what they did not know to be forbidden, and this type is plentiful. Ibn ‘Ajibah said: “It is often said on the tongues of the religious that lawful wealth is a lost stray, or non-existent. Stating this absolutely is an excuse for license and taking whatever comes into one’s hands. Rather, lawful wealth exists; if it were not available in every time, we would not be charged to seek it, nor would the friends of God have died of hunger, for that is their sustenance—and that is false. If the whole is forbidden, the whole is permitted; everyone who has something must examine it according to God’s ruling.”

From Ibn ‘Ajibah’s words: “If wealth is lacking, seek it through ten things: trade with honesty, hiring with sincere advice, unowned wild plants, a gift from a righteous brother, hunting on land where permitted, hunting in the sea, women’s bridal gifts given willingly, a share of spoils distributed according to the law, inheritance, and asking when in need.” I say: Al-Ghazali has a precious discussion on the issues of earning in his *Ihya’*; let the reader consult it. Lawful wealth can also be obtained through other ways than those mentioned by the shaykh.

Some scholars said: “Unlawful wealth does not pass beyond two debts.” If unlawful wealth reaches a person, and he does not know its exact origin, and then the ownership of this wealth is transferred to him through a lawful means, even if long, then this wealth is lawful for him in their view. Therefore, most scholars hold that one should not be meticulous in asking about the origin of things; they went so far as to say that the lawful is what whose origin is unknown.

> “They avoided the food of the oppressive, the rebellious, and the corrupt, fearing harm.”

Ibn ‘Ajibah said: “The people of oppression are unjust kings and those forced to labor for them; the people of rebellion are thieves and highway robbers; the people of corruption are those who deal in riba and corrupt transactions, and do not avoid the unlawful.”

Shaykh Zarruq said: “Their avoidance of the food of oppressors and the like is for several reasons: First, what is in it of wrong, along with what accompanies it of injustice, unless there is fear of clear harm. Second, the domination over the weak by the people of oppression, either through bad suspicion born of ignorance, because they believe that what is in their hands is forbidden and that whoever eats it has no share, so they belittle that person, indeed everyone of his kind, using him as a proof against others. Thus one may be unable to expand as he wishes in godliness, or to narrow the circle of one’s knowledge, so someone says to him: ‘So-and-so, who is greater than you, ate my food; who are you compared to him?’—and that harms. Third, that it helps them in what they are engaged in, for they then consider themselves not of the people of evil. Fourth, the inclination of the soul to them and its familiarity with them.”

Abu Nu‘aym related in his *Hilyah* that Ibn al-Mubarak entered upon the caliph, exhorted him, and reminded him; the caliph gave him money, and he bought slaves and freed them. Muhammad ibn Wasi‘ said to him about that: “You reminded them of God and exhorted them, and you took the wealth of God and spent it in His cause.” Ibn al-Mubarak said: “By God, has your heart now returned to what it was?” He said: “No.” So he sought forgiveness from God for all of them.

Fifth, that it involves consuming doubtful things without necessity. Shaykh Abu al-‘Abbas al-Mursi, may God be pleased with him, said: “Whoever among the poor of this time loves listening to music and eats the wealth of oppressors, has in him an inclination toward Judaism.” God said:

> “...They take usury, while they were forbidden from it...” [Quran, Al-Nisa’ 4:161]

Briefly. Sixth, the humiliation and change of state that result from this, which happen to many people. Some of the great ones made this a policy: if they saw a poor man who became strong against them through power, and they feared his call or the like, they would bestow favors upon him and scheme against him until he fell into their hands, so that he could not be proud against them. Some shaykhs of the Maghrib used to say: “The poor man does not walk by night nor flee by day if he sees something to fear, nor does he eat the food of oppressors.” I say: because all of these things beget humiliation. Seventh, that it opens the door to confusion, as people think he has standing with them, and they turn to him requesting intercession, a matter he cannot fulfill. A man became attached to one of them and asked for help in his case—and God knows best. All of this is when there is no necessity, and the person knows his own situation.

> “Rather, they ate only what its lawfulness was clear, not what they did not know the origin of.”

Ibn ‘Ajibah said: “That is, the people would not eat except what was clearly lawful and whose permissibility was verified; they would not eat what they did not know the origin of—whether it was lawful or unlawful. This is perhaps connected to the presence of doubt and uncertainty.” I say: we have discussed this subject before, so let the reader review it.

> “They did not hate conversation because of it, but they hated forcing one another.”

Ibn ‘Ajibah said: “Conversation about food is good, because silence about food indicates greed and gluttony. It is recommended that it be with knowledge or with stories of the righteous, and that it be after swallowing food, not while chewing, lest something come out of the mouth and fall into the food, disgusting others. So the eater should not speak as long as food is in his mouth.” Some shaykhs considered it recommended to say the Name of God at every morsel and to praise Him upon swallowing it. Ibn al-Hajj said: “This is a good thing, but the Sunnah does not mention it, and it is better than everything else.” So the people did not hate conversation during eating; rather, they hated *al-irgham*—that is, forcing one’s companions to eat, because of the affectation in that and the like. The proper manner in that is to leave each person to eat what he wishes. Your saying to him “Eat” may cause him to become distracted by some work or excuse in order to give them a chance to take their freedom.

They disliked eating twice in a day, or once in two days. Here “day” means daytime. Ibn ‘Ajibah said: “By ‘day’ is meant the brightness of day from dawn to sunset.” And he said: “It is understood from the poet’s words that the praiseworthy is eating once in a day—meaning once in the daytime and once at night—and this is the middle course; eating once in two days is neglect, and eating three times a day is excess.” Shaykh Zarruq said: “This is the judgment for one whose temperament is balanced or close to it. As for one inclined to excess or neglect, he should not abandon his judgment, but should do what corrects him without neglect or departure from the truth. Excessive satiety that spoils the stomach and wastes food without need is forbidden; what burdens the limbs without spoiling anything is disliked without disagreement. It is best for a person not to eat until moderately hungry—that is, he desires what will sustain him, which is his usual amount of food. He should not wait until he desires every kind of food, for that is harmful to thought and weakens strength. Nor should he eat merely with appetite, which is seeking food accompanied by desire.”

I say: One may take two meals in the twenty-four hours by analogy with fasting: a pre-dawn meal and a breaking-of-fast meal. And God said in describing the people of Paradise:

> “They will have whatever they desire therein, morning and evening.” [Quran, Maryam 19:62]

We should note that what matters is not that a meal be at night or in the day; in some lands, night may last twenty-three hours. So the point is that we have two meals in the twenty-four hours. This belongs to manners. We also observe in the life of the Arabs before Islam and after it that they had two drinkings: the morning drink, called *sabah*, and the night drink, called *ghabuq*. Their drink was milk. Derivations of *ghabuq* occur in Sunnah texts, and it is reported in sound Sunnah that the Messenger of God ﷺ used to drink at the last part of the night. People in our time have become accustomed to drinking tea and coffee, with milk or without, at many times. If a person can have two main meals in the twenty-four hours, and two supplementary drinks in the twenty-four hours, with moderation in all of that, then I hope there is no objection. There is no doubt that the people of our time have expanded in food and drink, to the point that obesity has appeared among them and diseases have afflicted them. Therefore, a return to the Sunnah in the matter of food is necessary. Undoubtedly, many meals are not of the Sunnah; but there are medical conditions for which multiple meals are necessary. Let that be observed, and let one observe the general manners of the Muslim in this matter and others.

When one is invited, that has its manners; ordinary eating has its manners; exceptional situations have their manners; and extravagance is always forbidden or disliked according to its degree. They preferred eating in a group over eating alone because of the increase in hands; so they preferred eating together over eating alone, in realization of the Sunnah of increasing hands over food. In that there is physical and spiritual blessing, as well as exercise in chastity and avoidance of greed and gluttony, because a person eating alone is a sign of stinginess, greed, or gluttony—unless there is a legal or ordinary necessity. One should observe whom one eats with. Al-Junayd said: “Eating together is an entering into intimacy, so look at whom you eat with, and let not one person put a morsel into another’s mouth.” That is, the Sufis did not have the custom of feeding one another morsels in jest, because of the lack of modesty and reverence in that. But if it is done as a means of blessing or friendliness, there is no objection; indeed, sometimes it may be the manner of the occasion.

> “Let not their glance in it be except lowered.”

Among their manners is that they would not direct their eyes toward those eating with them; rather, they would lower their eyes and look in front of them, because looking around embarrasses the eaters, especially since a person’s state while eating is a kind of uncoveredness, particularly if he is elderly.

> “Let them not wait in it, so that time passes without remembrance.”

In this line, he points out that the Sufis’ doctrine when food arrived was to hasten to eat, and they did not wait for whoever was absent. Rather, they would set aside his right and eat, so that time would not be wasted in idleness. I say: this is when there is no inconvenience, or when there is an appointed time.

> “And they disliked satiety for the brothers.”

*Al-batnah* is fullness of the stomach from food. The author informs us that the Sufis disliked fullness, or excess beyond what the body needs, because of the dislike and harm in that. “For in it there is an opening for Satan.” This alludes to the *hadith*: “Satan flows through the son of Adam like blood.” The author means that Satan, through filling the stomach, reaches many of his goals with a person; it is as if the stomach is the vessel in which Satan places his wishes for the person.

> “And in it they commanded that the door be opened.”

That is, opening the door of the house where they are eating, so that anyone in need of food may enter. This is from their generosity and the richness of their hearts. They do not drive away those who come to them; rather, they receive them and rejoice in them, and they may even see it as a favor upon them that they eat with them—rather, they consider it a gift from God to them, especially if it is one of their brothers or someone in need. The matter, in any case, belongs to manners; but there may be obstacles stronger than the manner that prevent a person from applying it. The legal ruling depends on time, place, and person. For example, one who has prepared food for a specific number and cannot provide for more than them—their right takes precedence over the rights of others.

> “And they ate with moderation and manners.”

Eating with moderation means without excess or neglect: one does not reach habitual satiety, but falls short of it, yet not to the point of harming the body. One should not make the morsel too large, but small. Eating with manners means observing every manner: mentioning the Name of God aloud at the beginning, intending strength for obedience to God, washing the hands—especially if dirty—eating on the ground if possible, not on a raised table; sitting on one leg, the left one, and raising the other, pressing it against the stomach if one can; eating from what is nearest, not differing from that; making the morsel small; chewing well; not looking at one’s companion’s morsel. It is not mannerly to lick one’s fingers before finishing eating and then return them to the dish; nor to lean over the food so that something falls from one’s mouth; nor to shake one’s hand in the dish. Among the established manners are glorifying God after finishing, licking the fingers if one ate with them and washing them, wiping the hands and mouth, washing them after eating, picking up what has fallen from the food, eating with the right hand except when the left assists the right, not moving the hand around except with one’s family, children, and where moving it is permitted, and opening the door to every passerby. This confirms what we mentioned before.

> “And they ate with gentleness and preferring others.”

Gentleness in eating means taking one’s time so that the morsel cools, not raising another until one has swallowed what is in one’s mouth, chewing well, masticating the food thoroughly, not showing greed or craving, but showing contentment and independence from it. Eating with preferring others means giving others precedence over oneself if the food is little, or if there is something desirable, sharing it with others.

We conclude this chapter by recalling that among good manners is accompanying the guest to the door of the house. And with the words of ‘Abd al-Rahman al-Sani’, who said: “Some of the Sufi shaykhs said: The host has three duties, and the guest has three duties. As for the host: to feed him from the lawful, to preserve the prayer times for him, and not to withhold from him any food he is able to provide. As for the guest: to sit where he is placed, to be content with what is offered, and not to leave except with permission.”

## Section: On Some of Their Manners Regarding Listening

We have seen that spiritual audition (*sama‘*) stirs the soul to wayfaring and assists it, and that it has many services; therefore, the Sufis cultivated it. We have discussed this subject before, clarifying its benefits and drawbacks. We have seen that the basis of listening for the Companions of the Messenger of God ﷺ was hearing the Qur’an, and that whatever else occurred was incidental, and in relation to it like salt in food. They discussed in their books *sama‘* and its manners; therefore, the author of *al-Mabahith al-Asliyah* devoted a passage to it. It seems that part of this passage concerns their manners in listening; let us quote some of this part, with some comments, drawing on Ibn ‘Ajibah’s commentary.

The author of *al-Mabahith* said:

> “There is no idle talk in it, nor distraction, nor smiling.”

That is, idle talk during *sama‘* is not appropriate, because conversation removes one from the purpose of *sama‘*. If the session of *sama‘* is for wisdom, this wisdom is nullified by the presence of conversation. Then he said: “Nor distraction, nor smiling.” Distraction from it indicates lack of manners in it, which requires that one not attend it in the first place. As for smiling during it, it may suggest contempt, disdain, ridicule, or the like. In sum, we say: sessions of *sama‘* are like stimulating medicines, and a person is between two options: either attend them and give them their due, or not attend them at all.

> “And shouting in it, and tearing clothes, and shaking the head, and clapping, are weakness.”

That is, shouting, tearing clothes, moving the head, and striking one hand against the other are all signs of weakness of inner conduct. What has preceded was mentioned in relation to the beginner who is overcome by states; as for the strong one who masters the states, nothing of that issues from him. I say: If such things are considered weakness, then how much more so what is greater than them! It is time for the wayfarers to God to discipline their behavior, so that they are not a cause of disapproval among the common and the elite. It is time for a spiritual life disciplined within the limits that the Companions, may God be pleased with them, were upon; within these limits, we do not care what any critic says. As for what exceeds these limits, it is time for us to force ourselves to abandon it, thereby showing mercy to ourselves and to the Muslims.

> “And no gathering should be for it, nor dispersion at its absence.”

That is because *sama‘* is not a pillar of the path, nor a condition of it; if it is present, it is so, and if it is absent, it is not missed. It is not the axis of gathering. Unfortunately, for many Sufis *sama‘* has become what gathers them, so that the singer has become the center of gathering, not the shaykh, nor wayfaring to God. This is removing things from their proper places.

Then the author mentioned how the listening of the people did not involve instruments:

> “And there were no crooners in it.”

That is, no people who hum along, as is the habit of the people of amusement when the singer finishes his song; they hum to show sympathy and harmony.

> “Nor lutes and listeners.”

*Al-tanabir*: plural of *tanbur*, resembling the *‘ud* in form; some say it is the same. *Al-sama‘un*: those who monitor singing in gatherings and make people hear their singing. So the chanting of the Sufis is unpretentious, and is not accompanied by what accompanies singing of instruments and customs.

> “Nor tambourines, nor drumming.”

*Al-tar*: what has small cymbals. *Al-mazahir*: plural of *mizhar*, a skin instrument without jingles. *Al-tanqar* in the line means the act of striking, so whatever is called striking is not present in their circles, whether it be drumming on a drum, striking a goblet—which people today call a *darbuka*—or plucking an *‘ud*.

> “And candles, and carpets, and pretension—by God, they were not in their state.”

That is, they did not affect pretension in *sama‘* to the point of bringing lit candles, spread carpets, and decorated cushions. Rather, they attended it in poverty and simplicity, according to what the time and situation offered. This does not mean that such things are forbidden; rather, he means that the way of the people is absence of pretension.

Then the author mentioned the origin of the rise of *sama‘* among them and the reasons for its existence. After that, he mentioned that among their manners is that sessions of *sama‘* should conclude with *dhikr* and explanation of what was recited:

> “And if the reciter continues and the poets are finished.”

That is, if the singer continues his singing until he finishes the poem, “they open a book of commentary upon him.” *Al-sifr* is the book. The meaning is that after the singing they engage in *dhikr* and explanation, so that the recitation is placed in its proper meanings, and the listeners ascend to the highest degrees of perceiving the realities, and their ambition is stirred toward attaining the stations.

## Section: Selected Directives of Ibn ‘Ata’

- “Among the signs of habituation to action is the decrease of hope when slips occur.”
- “Your striving in what is incumbent upon you, and your falling short in what is requested of you, is proof of the dimming of your insight.”
- “Actions are lifeless forms; their spirit is the presence of sincerity in them.”
- “Your longing to uncover the faults hidden within you is better than your thinking about what is veiled from you.”
- “Whoever does not thank blessings exposes himself to their removal; and whoever is not roused to them is among the signs of self-deception.”
- “If your way becomes confused to you and you are not afraid for it of the dominance of death over you...”
- “Cling to the attributes of His Lordship, and be realized in the attributes of your servitude. Your being prevented from claiming what is not yours with created beings—how then can you claim His attribute, He Who is the Lord of the Worlds?”
- “People praise you without knowing you; so be the one who censures yourself for what you know of it. When the believer is praised, he feels shy before God that his attribute should be applied to him which he does not witness in himself. The most ignorant of people is one who leaves the certainty of what he has for the supposition of what people have. If unrestricted praise is directed at you and you are not worthy of it, praise Him to whom it is truly worthy.”
- “If a sin occurs from you, do not let it be a cause of despair in attaining uprightness with your Lord; for it may be the last sin decreed for you.”
- “Your looking forward to the creation knowing your particular distinction is proof of the lack of your sincerity in servitude.”
- “The best knowledge is that accompanied by fear; if knowledge is accompanied by fear, it is for you, otherwise it is against you.”
- “Whoever establishes humility for himself is truly arrogant; for humility is only from elevation. So if you establish humility for yourself, you are the arrogant one. The humble person is not one who, when he is humble, sees himself above what he did; but the humble person is one who, when he is humble, sees himself below what he did. True humility is what arises from witnessing His greatness and the manifestation of His attributes.”

## Section: On Comprehensive Character Traits

In our book *Jund Allah Thaqafatan wa Akhlaqan* (“The Army of God in Culture and Character”), we mentioned that the fundamental character traits of the Muslim, to which every character trait returns, are what God mentioned in the verses of the pledge in Surah al-Ma’idah:

> “O you who have believed, whoever of you should revert from his religion—Allah will bring forth a people He will love and who will love Him, humble toward the believers, mighty toward the disbelievers; they strive in the cause of Allah and do not fear the blame of a critic. That is the unbounded favor of Allah; He gives it to whom He wills. And Allah is all-Encompassing and Knowing. Your ally is none but Allah and His Messenger and those who have believed—those who establish prayer and give zakah, and they bow in worship.” [Quran, Al-Ma’idah 5:54-55]

These verses mention noble character traits that are the foundation of the morality of the party of God. Any neglect of any of these character traits means some deviation from this lofty morality—and how numerous are those who are negligent.

We refer the reader to that book. And in the treatise *Min Ajl Khatwah ila al-Amam ‘ala Tarîq al-Jihâd al-Mubârak* (“For a Step Forward on the Path of Blessed Struggle”), we highlighted that the characteristics of the Islamic rank are defined by the verses of Surah al-Shura:

> “And those who respond to their Lord and establish prayer, whose affair is consultation among them, and who spend of what We have provided them; and those who, when tyranny strikes them, defend themselves; and the recompense of an evil is an evil like it—but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation, his reward is due from Allah. Indeed, He does not like the wrongdoers. And whoever defends himself after he has been wronged, upon those there is no cause. The cause is only against those who wrong people and cause corruption in the land unjustly; for them will be a painful punishment. And whoever is patient and forgives—indeed, that is of the matters requiring resolve.” [Quran, Al-Shura 42:38-43]

Note that the Qur’an gathered in these verses a number of attributes of the Islamic rank, and how great is the negligence of Muslims in them. Note that defending oneself from oppression and oppressors is one of the characteristics of the Islamic rank. Al-Nasafi said: “They used to dislike humiliating themselves so that the wicked would dare against them.” Note that defense should be within the limits of the known proof. Note the error of people in blaming the oppressed if he defends himself, and not blaming the oppressor for his aggression. God said:

> “And whoever defends himself after he has been wronged, upon those there is no cause. The cause is only against those who wrong the people and cause corruption in the land without right.” [Quran, Al-Shura 42:41-42]

These are scattered sections in which we have drawn attention in each to manners, character traits, or behavioral rulings. We did not intend comprehensiveness; rather, we intended to draw attention to the issue of manners and character in *tasawwuf* in particular, and in Islam in general, so that its place may be known. For although this treatise is a signpost on the path, it would be a deficiency if it did not contain some of these matters. In the next chapter we will mention scattered sections that we consider ought to be addressed in any book on *tasawwuf*, even if brief; hence the next chapter is on “Miscellaneous Sections.”
