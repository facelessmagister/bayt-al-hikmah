# Chapter 5: Litanies and the Atmosphere of the Verse of Light

> Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth. The example of His light is like a niche within which is a lamp, the lamp is within glass, the glass as if it were a pearly [white] star lit from [the oil of] a blessed olive tree, neither of the east nor of the west, whose oil would almost glow even if untouched by fire. Light upon light. Allah guides to His light whom He wills. And Allah presents examples for the people, and Allah is Knowing of all things. [In houses] which Allah has ordered to be raised and that His name be mentioned therein; exalting Him within them in the morning and the afternoon. [Qur'an, An-Nur 24:35-36]

> [Are] men whom neither commerce nor sale distracts from the remembrance of Allah and performance of prayer and giving of zakah. They fear a Day in which the hearts and eyes will [fearfully] turn about. [Qur'an, An-Nur 24:37]

> That Allah may reward them [according to] the best of what they did and increase them from His bounty. And Allah gives provision to whom He wills without account [i.e., limit]. [Qur'an, An-Nur 24:38]

Understanding these verses is among the greatest aids to understanding the issue of hearts and the issue of journeying to Allah, the Exalted and Majestic. We shall try to present them as simply as we are able.

In the first verse, one of its components is the *Mishkāh* (مشكاة), the lamp, and the glass. The *Mishkāh* is the cavity in the wall that is not a window, the lamp is the burning light, and the glass is the lantern that contains the luminous lamp. What do these three parts correspond to in the parable? They correspond in the believer to three things: his body, his heart, and the light present in this heart. Thus the body corresponds to the *Mishkāh*, the heart corresponds to the glass, and the light corresponds to the lamp placed inside the glass. Our evidence for what we hold—that the believer's body corresponds to the *Mishkāh*, his heart to the glass, and the light in his heart to the lamp inside the glass—is what Ibn Kathir reported: Jaʿfar al-Rāzī related from al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, from Abū al-ʿĀliyah, from ʿUbayy ibn Kaʿb, concerning the saying of Allah, the Exalted: *"Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth. The example of His light is..."* He said: It is the believer, in whose breast Allah has placed faith and the Qur'an; so Allah struck his parable and said: *"Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth,"* beginning with the light of His own self, then He mentioned the light of the believer and said: *"The example of the light of one who believes in Him..."* ʿUbayy ibn Kaʿb used to recite it as *"The example of the light of one who believes in Him."* So he is the believer in whose breast Allah has placed faith and the Qur'an.

This is also reported by Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and Qays ibn Saʿd from Ibn ʿAbbās, that he likewise recited it: *"The example of the light of one who believes in Allah."* From this transmission we realize that what we have held is correct: that *"Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth"* means that He is the Guide; there is no guidance in the heavens and the earth except by His light, exalted is His majesty. Then He struck a parable of His guiding things by His light, through the guidance of the believer, and in this parable He struck mighty examples so that the greatness of His guidance and the sublimity of His affair might be made clear. Therefore the *Mishkāh* is the body of the believer, which contains his heart; the glass is the heart of the believer, which contains the light of the heart by which the believer is guided, so that he sees things as they truly are and walks by the guidance of his Lord because of this light. This is the first stage in this parable.

Then comes the second stage in the parable: this glass that contains the lamp—that is, this heart that contains the light—is likened, in the intensity of its light, to a brilliant star that resembles a pearl in its radiance and qualities. Notice here that He combined the description of the glass and its lamp—that is, the heart and its light—by likening both together to a brilliant star; for the lamp is luminous, and the glass itself is luminous because of its clarity and purity. This is the second stage in the parable.

Then comes the third stage: This lamp in the glass, from where is it kindled? From where does it draw its light? How does its luminosity endure? Or we may say: This light in the heart, or this luminous heart, from where does it draw its luminosity? What is the continuous sustenance that comes to it? What is the source of this light? Allah, the Exalted, says: *"Lit from [the oil of] a blessed olive tree, neither of the east nor of the west"*—that is, the oil of this lamp in the glass, that is, the light present in the heart of the believer, comes *"from a blessed olive tree,"* that is, an abundant, beneficial olive tree, *"neither of the east nor of the west."* Al-Nasafī said: "Meaning, it is not from the east nor from the west, but from the middle of them."

This olive tree here is the *sharīʿah* (شريعة) of Allah, the Exalted and Majestic. Ibn Kathir said: "He likened the believer in the purity of his self to a lantern of transparent, gem-like glass, and what corresponds to it of the Qur'an and the *sharīʿah* to good, clear, radiant, balanced oil in which there is no turbidity or crookedness." Note Ibn Kathir's words: "and what corresponds to it of the Qur'an and the *sharīʿah* to good, clear, radiant oil." Thus the olive tree here is the *sharīʿah* of Allah; it is neither eastern nor western, but heavenly and pure. In our age we understand the meaning of Allah's *sharīʿah* being neither eastern nor western more broadly than those before us understood it, after the East came to be associated with the communists and the West with the capitalists. This is the third stage of the parable.

Then comes the fourth stage of the parable: This blessed tree from which the heart draws its light—this beneficial *sharīʿah* from which the heart draws—Al-Nasafī said: "He described the oil with purity and gleaming, so that because of its radiance it would almost glow even without fire." How great is the luminosity of this *sharīʿah* that supplies the heart with light! And how great is the light of this heart that draws its luminosity from this *sharīʿah*! For that reason Allah, the Exalted, says: *"Light upon light."* This is the fifth stage of the parable. Al-Suddi said: "That is, this light that Allah has likened to His affair is a doubled light, in which the *Mishkāh*, the glass, the lamp, and the oil reinforce one another until no trace remains that could strengthen the light." This is because when the lamp is in a narrow place like the *Mishkāh*, its light is more intense, whereas in a wide place the light spreads and the lantern is the greatest aid to increasing illumination; likewise the oil and its purity. Ibn Kathir said: "Al-Ḥasan, concerning Allah's saying *"Light upon light,"* said: The light of the fire and the light of the oil, when they come together, give light, and one does not shine without the other. Likewise the light of the Qur'an and the light of faith, when they come together—one is not complete without the other." Note his words: "Likewise the light of the Qur'an and the light of faith, when they come together." This brings to an end the parable that Allah, the Exalted and Majestic, has struck to clarify the nature of His guidance and its greatness.

From this parable we understand that acting by the *sharīʿah* of Allah is what supplies the light of faith with continuous sustenance. We have seen in the last word of al-Suddi on this subject where he said: "The light of the lamp and the light of the oil, when they come together, give light, and one does not shine without the other. Likewise the light of the Qur'an and the light of faith, when they come together—one is not complete without the other." From this we know that acting by the Qur'an is the continuous sustenance of the heart by which the lamp of the heart remains lit, by which man remains guided, and according to the extent that man acts by this Qur'an, the light of his heart increases in intensity and radiance, and the *Mishkāh*—that is, the body—reflects this light, illuminating the road for the one who possesses it and for others as well.

Let us continue presenting the verse. From what has passed we recognize the greatness of Allah's guidance and the radiance of His light, but why do some people remain in disbelief? The answer is that these do not desire Allah to guide them. Therefore Allah, the Exalted, says: *"Allah guides to His light whom He wills. And Allah presents examples for the people, and Allah is Knowing of all things"*—that is, He guides to the light of His *sharīʿah*, or Allah guides whom He wills among the people of faith so that they may take from Him and be guided by His guidance. Then the second verse comes to show us where we find these people whose hearts are of this kind in guidance and illumination. Allah, the Exalted, says: *"[In houses] which Allah has ordered to be raised and that His name be mentioned therein."* Al-Nasafī said: "That is, like a *Mishkāh* in some of the houses of Allah, which are the mosques." And the *Mishkāh* is the body of the believer. Therefore this kind of hearts and their possessors are most likely to be found in the mosques, and from this we realize that the starting point of lofty Islamic education is the mosques.

The verses continue in describing this kind of people: *"Exalting Him therein"*—that is, in the mosques—*"in the morning and the afternoon"*—that is, by performing prayer in them, the dawn prayer and others—*"men whom neither commerce nor sale distracts from the remembrance of Allah and performance of prayer and giving of zakah. They fear a Day in which the hearts and eyes will [fearfully] turn about."* The verse has told us the nature of the acts by which the luminous sustenance of the heart is obtained: glorification (*tasbīḥ*) (تسبيح), *dhikr* (ذكر), establishing prayer, giving *zakāh* (زكاة), and fear of what will occur on the Last Day. Then our Lord, the Exalted and Majestic, made clear how He will honor these people, saying: *"That Allah may reward them [according to] the best of what they did and increase them from His bounty. And Allah gives provision to whom He wills without account [i.e., limit]."*

Before we begin to explain the purpose for which we introduced this discussion of these verses, we would like to record some incidental observations:

1. One of the professors at the University of Damascus, known to have had leftist inclinations, wrote a study of candles and lamps in world literature and concluded that nothing recorded in the history of literature describing candles and lamps is more eloquent than the verse, *"Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth."*

2. We notice from these verses the importance of mosque-based education (*tarbiyyah*) (تربية), and that the correct Islamic beginning is the one that starts from the mosque. Concerning this is the hadith: "If you see a man frequenting the mosques, then testify to his faith." [Reported by Ahmad, al-Tirmidhi, and Ibn Majah; it is a sahih hadith.]

3. Some people have composed lengthy treatises on these verses, so we hope no one will imagine that we have given them their due of research. The fact is that we have mentioned in their interpretation only what helps in understanding the matter of this epistle.

After this, what did we discuss these two verses for? We discussed them in order to know the connection between acting by the *sharīʿah* and the luminosity of the heart, and to know that acting by the *sharīʿah* has effects on the heart, and that every kind of righteous deed has its luminous effects (*wāridāt*) (واردات), and that there are particular acts whose effects in bestowal are most exalted, which is why the verses singled them out for mention: attachment to mosques, abundant *dhikr*, *tasbīḥ*, establishing prayers, giving *zakāh*, and fear of the Hereafter. So whoever hopes that his heart may be illumined without his having deeds and *awrād* (أوراد) has not left the houses of his forefathers in vain hope.

Perhaps from what has passed we have realized the idea of the *wird* (ورد) and the *wārid* (وارد) that the *ṣūfiyyah* (صوفية) often speak of. A person's *wird* is what he arranges for himself of types of obedience and worship, while the *wārid* is what Allah, the Exalted and Majestic, bestows upon a person's heart of outpourings, lights, and meanings. If we understand the matter of the *wird* and the *wārid*, we understand the necessity that a person have his daily *awrād*. In what follows we shall quote some of Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh al-Iskandarī's expressions concerning the *wird* and the *wārid*, and comment on them so that some aspects of this subject may become clear through the speech of the *ṣūfiyyah*, after we have seen what the texts indicate concerning it.

Ibn ʿAṭāʾ said: "The varieties of deeds are diverse because the effects received by the states are diverse."

I say: Allah, the Exalted and Majestic, has imposed upon the Muslim diverse obligatory duties and required of him many deeds, because the human heart needs diverse kinds of effects. Every deed leaves its traces in the heart if the intention is sound, and the rectitude of the heart lies in performing all the deeds. Every deed produces a kind of state in the heart, and every state needs a kind of righteous deed in order to be...

And Ibn ʿAṭāʾ said: "Among the signs of following caprice is rushing to supererogatory good works while being sluggish in performing the obligatory duties."

I say: In this is an indication that the Muslim must not neglect an obligatory duty in favor of a supererogatory one. This is a matter most people are heedless of; most people are ignorant of the obligatory duties of the time—and how numerous they are—and occupy themselves with matters that belong to the category of the permissible, and some of them belong to the category of innovations, while they imagine that they are doing something good.

And Ibn ʿAṭāʾ said: "If you see a servant whom Allah, the Exalted, has established by the presence of *awrād* and whom He has kept constant upon them along with prolonged sustenance, then do not disdain what his Master has given him, for you do not see upon him the mark of the knowers nor the radiance of the lovers; had there not been a *wārid*, there would not have been a *wird*."

From the shaykh's words it is understood that whenever a *wird* exists, a *wārid* exists too, whether its possessor feels it or not, whether others sense it or not. The shaykh has clarified the importance of the *wird* for the human being, and he cautions against some ignorant *ṣūfiyyah* who disdain the people of *awrād* when certain meanings do not appear upon them.

He said, emphasizing the importance of the *wird*: "No one disdains the *wird* except one who is ignorant of the *wārid*. The *wārid* will be found in the Abode to Come, and the *wird* will be folded up with the folding up of this abode; the first thing one should tend to is what will not leave off existing... The arrivals of sustenance are according to the capacity to receive, and the dawning of lights is according to the purity of the innermost hearts."

And he said: "The places where the lights rise are the hearts, and the innermost hearts are a light stored in the hearts; its supply is from the light that comes from the treasuries of the unseen: a light by which He discloses to you His effects, and a light by which He discloses to you His attributes." This passage points to kinds of divine effects that descend upon the heart and the traces they leave in it.

And he said, clarifying kinds of states that are kinds of effects: "If you desire the arrival of gifts upon you, then rectify poverty and need [for Allah] in yourself; by realizing that, He will sustain you with His might; by realizing your incapacity, He will sustain you with His power; by realizing your weakness, He will sustain you with His majesty and strength."

And he said: "There are people whose lights precede their *adhkhār*; there are people whose *adhkhār* precede their lights; there are people whose *adhkhār* and lights are equal; and there are people who have neither *adhkhār* nor lights—we seek refuge with Allah from that. One person remembers and his heart is illumined, so he is a rememberer; another remembers and his heart is illumined, so he is a rememberer; and the one whose *adhkhār* and lights are equal, his *dhikr* guides him and his light guides him." He enjoined the people of *dhikr* not to abandon their *awrād* because of the persistence of the heedlessness of hearts: "Do not abandon *dhikr* because of your absence from Allah in it, for your heedlessness of the existence of His *dhikr* is more severe than your heedlessness in the existence of *dhikr*. Perhaps He will raise you from *dhikr* with the presence of heedlessness to *dhikr* with the presence of wakefulness; and from *dhikr* with the presence of wakefulness to *dhikr* with the presence of presence; and from *dhikr* with the presence of presence to *dhikr* with the presence of absence from other than the Remembered; and that is not difficult for Allah."

And he said, explaining the wisdom of the multiplicity of acts of obedience in the *sharīʿah*: "Because Allah knew in you the existence of boredom, He diversified the acts of obedience for you; and because He knew in you the existence of gluttony, He restricted them for you at certain times."

I say: Allah restricted prayer upon us at sunrise, and He restricted us from fasting on the two ʿĪd days and the days of Tashrīq.

And he said, clarifying the place of prayer and the importance of its effects: "Prayer is a purifier of the heart from the filth of sins, and an opening of the door of the unseen; prayer is the place of intimate converse and the mine of sincerity, in which the fields of the innermost hearts expand and in which the flashes of lights rise. According to the weakness in you, lessen its number; and according to your need for His favor, increase its sustenance."

I say: This human heart needs medicines and nourishments; in prayer there is medicine and nourishment, in fasting there is medicine and nourishment, in *dhikr* there is medicine and nourishment, in *jihād* (جهاد) there is medicine and nourishment, in maintaining family ties there is medicine and nourishment, and in knowledge there is medicine and nourishment. All of this is, for the prophets, nourishment and elevations. Perhaps in this chapter we have realized the importance of *awrād* in the life of the Muslim and their importance in the rectification and elevation of his heart, so let us move on to the next chapter.
