# Chapter 1: General Islamic Introduction

## Islam

Al-Ustādh al-Bannā said: “A comprehensive system that deals with all aspects of life; it is a state and a homeland — or a government and a nation; it is morality and strength — or right and justice; it is culture and law — or knowledge and judiciary; it is matter and wealth — or earnings and riches; it is *jihād* (جهاد) and *daʿwah* (دعوة) — or an army and an idea; it is a true creed and a sound worship, equal to one another.” He also said, may God have mercy on him: “For we believe that Islam is a complete whole that regulates all affairs of life, gives a ruling in every matter, and sets for it a precise, wise system; it does not stand idle before the vital formations and systems that are indispensable for reforming people.”

What al-Ustādh al-Bannā said about Islam is the very truth regarding Islam. It is among the most self-evident truths that has been absent from the minds of many Muslims, let alone others, although the texts of the Qur’an are clear in this regard. God — Exalted is He — says:

> And We have sent down to you the Book as clarification for all things and as guidance and mercy and good tidings for the Muslims.
>
> [Qur’an, an-Nahl 16:89]

The word “clarification for all things” clearly indicates that the Qur’an has covered the whole of human life by giving it the sufficient answer concerning the affairs of conduct in every matter. Thus the Qur’an covers human life either by a direct answer, or by the words of the Messenger — peace and blessings be upon him — his deeds and his states, which are an explanation of the Qur’an, or by what the Book and the Sunnah refer to of methods through which the rulings of Islam are derived for ordinary circumstances and exceptional circumstances, so that they encompass time and place, persons and conditions. These are numerous topics of which many have been heedless or which many have failed to understand.

Just as many people have been heedless of or have ignored the issue of the comprehensiveness of Islam, so they have also ignored or overlooked another issue: the issue of *īmān* (إيمان). For belief in Islam as a whole is a condition for a person to be considered a Muslim. If deficiency in understanding Islam is flawed, it is natural that the issue of belief itself should be flawed.

Much confusion often arises concerning the relationship between Islam and īmān, and much error occurs in understanding the texts that mention īmān and Islam. Therefore it is necessary to clarify these matters.

The word “Islam” is used for the religion that God — Mighty and Majestic is He — revealed to Muḥammad — peace and blessings be upon him — and whose details are established by the texts of the Book and the Sunnah. In this sense — in our view — it is a complete, comprehensive system that encompasses all matters of human life. It contains doctrines, acts of worship, and laws; it has its supports, so it is doctrines, laws, and rites; it is a complete, comprehensive covering for the affairs of this world and the Hereafter, encompassing time and place.

The word “Islam” is also used as a description for a person who has entered into Islam; one says: So-and-so is a Muslim, meaning that he has entered into Islam. And one says: “The *islām* of so-and-so means the submission of so-and-so to this religion.” Hence the word “Islam” is also applied to action. If a person’s heart and limbs submit to God in everything with which God has charged him, outwardly and inwardly, then that is the submission of truth. God — Exalted is He — says:

> So turn your face toward the religion, inclining to truth. That is the fitrah of Allah upon which He has created people.
>
> [Qur’an, ar-Rūm 30:30]

If a person’s limbs submit while his heart does not, then that is the hypocrite as long as he remains so.

As for īmān, it is sometimes applied to mere belief of the heart together with assent; sometimes it is applied to belief of the heart and what that belief entails of outward effects; and that is complete īmān, which settles in the heart and is confirmed by action. God — Exalted is He — says:

> The believers are those who, when Allah is mentioned, their hearts become fearful, and when His verses are recited to them, it increases them in faith; and upon their Lord they rely — the ones who establish prayer and spend from what We have provided them. Those are the believers, truly.
>
> [Qur’an, al-Anfāl 8:2–4]

And He says:

> The believers are only those who have believed in Allah and His Messenger and then doubt not but strive with their properties and their lives in the cause of Allah. It is those who are the truthful.
>
> [Qur’an, al-Ḥujurāt 49:15]

Thus complete īmān is: the belief and assent of the heart together with the action of the limbs in accordance with what that requires. Complete īmān and complete Islam are therefore one and the same, since complete Islam is the submission of the heart and the limbs, and complete īmān is the confirmation of the heart and the confirmation of the limbs. Hence we find the Qur’an saying:

> So We brought out from therein whoever was of the believers. And We found not within it other than [one] house of Muslims.
>
> [Qur’an, adh-Dhāriyāt 51:35–36]

These, then, are Muslims and believers; their īmān is identical with their Islam, and their Islam is identical with their īmān. They are complete believers and complete Muslims. Complete Islam is identical with complete īmān.

If īmān lags behind Islam — as when someone enters Islam and practices its deeds but the light of complete īmān does not reach his heart — God — Exalted is He — says:

> The bedouins say, "We have believed." Say, "You have not [yet] believed; but say [instead], 'We have submitted,' for faith has not yet entered your hearts."
>
> [Qur’an, al-Ḥujurāt 49:14]

## The Meanings

When these meanings became clear, we could understand why some matters are sometimes mentioned in the context of Islam, why the same matters are sometimes mentioned in the context of īmān, why some matters are mentioned in the context of īmān purely in the sense of assent, and why some matters are mentioned in the context of Islam in the sense of the action and submission of the limbs. In all these aspects there is a kind of confusion, or a kind of deficiency in understanding and conception.

If deficiency occurred in the conceptions of Islam, deficiency also occurred in the conceptions of the stations of spiritual wayfaring in the religion of God. Deficiency in practice in these very stations is a result of deficiency in the general conception. Indeed, under ordinary circumstances, if one accepts entry into the religion of God — Islam — one must know the reality of the religion of God, must know what the duty of the time requires in one’s regard, and must carry it out, either by performing a command or by refraining from something forbidden.

One’s practice in Islam will consequently be perfected, and the light of īmān in it will increase. Just as one increases in action, the light of īmān increases, until the heart ascends to the station of *iḥsān* (إحسان). [The Prophet said:] “Worship God as if you see Him, for if you do not see Him, indeed He sees you.” For the station of iḥsān is the pinnacle of the station of īmān, by virtue of the hadith: “The best of īmān is that you know that God is witness over you wherever you are.”

To the extent that īmān is perfected and one realizes the station of iḥsān, that will be reflected upon one’s *sulūk* (سلوك) as uprightness, knowledge, and excellence. Thus one realizes *taqwā* (تقوى), which is God’s gift to His servants. God — Exalted is He — says:

> And those who are guided — He increases them in guidance and gives them their righteousness.
>
> [Qur’an, Muḥammad 47:17]

To the extent that one persists in the taqwā of God, we become performers of the right of gratitude, and we are on the path of ascending in it. It is the highest and most refined of stations. God — Exalted is He — says:

> Work, O family of David, in gratitude. And few of My servants are grateful.
>
> [Qur’an, Saba’ 34:13]

Taqwā is none other than the path leading to this station. God — Exalted is He — says:

> Fear Allah that you may be grateful.
>
> [Qur’an, Āl ʿImrān 3:123]

By the clarity of the issue of Islam and what is obligatory in it — what is a duty of time, which varies according to people’s conditions — and by the clarity of the issue of īmān, the practical, spiritual, and experiential aspects of the station of iḥsān become clear; by the clarity of the issue of iḥsān in its spiritual, experiential, and practical aspects, and by the clarity of the issue of taqwā in its spiritual, conceptual, and behavioral aspects, and by the clarity of the issue of gratitude in fulfilling the rights of complete servitude to God with thankfulness — by all of this, the wayfaring in the religion of God becomes sound.

These are great topics, and errors concerning them are many. Because of the abundance of errors, it is appropriate for us to discuss this matter at some length.

We have seen that Islam is the religion of God, and that God — Mighty and Majestic is He — has not left any issue without mentioning its ruling, either explicitly or by derivation. Thus Islam, in this sense, is the sum of God’s rulings in every issue: in doctrines, acts of worship, and systems of life. Īmān enters into Islam by the texts of the Book and the Sunnah and by the methods of deriving rulings from the Book and the Sunnah. Therefore Islam is something vast; it is enough to conceive of this vastness to consider this immense legacy of fiqh books numbering tens of thousands, and this immense legacy of books of *uṣūl al-fiqh* (أصول الفقه), books of doctrine, books of *taṣawwuf* (تصوف), and other works, including commentaries and explanations of the books of the Sunnah, and so on.

If this is Islam, then what is the human being charged with? What should each individual take from this religion? What are the stations of wayfaring in this religion to God — Mighty and Majestic is He?

Among the first duties of the person charged is to accept this religion and believe in it. Once he accepts it, he must begin to act in what is prescribed or recommended for him, and abandon what is forbidden or disliked for him. Thus he begins to learn, to become acquainted, and to take his share of prayer, *zakāh* (زكاة), fasting, and pilgrimage when the months of *ḥajj* (حج) come and he is obligated to perform it. He remembers God, restrains himself in earning so that he takes only what is lawful. This is his share of Islam in the sense of practical submission to God, as expressed in God’s saying:

> The bedouins say, "We have believed." Say, "You have not [yet] believed; but say [instead], 'We have submitted,' for faith has not yet entered your hearts."
>
> [Qur’an, al-Ḥujurāt 49:14]

From this verse we understand that a person’s persistence in performing the deeds of Islam qualifies him to take his share of the station of īmān in the heart. Note God’s words: “for faith has yet to enter your hearts.” The grammarians say that “*lamma*” indicates the expectation of the occurrence of what follows it, as in “faith has not yet entered your hearts,” meaning up to now they have not tasted it, but they will taste it. Apply this meaning to His words: “for faith has not yet entered your hearts,” and the meaning is: up to now. This is the interpretation of the verse in *Sūrat al-Ḥujurāt*.

It will enter, and it will enter if you persist in what you are upon. Note that it will enter into the hearts. By “hearts” here are meant the hearts in the breasts. God — Exalted is He — says:

> Rather, it has become clear to them what they previously concealed of it. And if they were returned, they would return to that which they were forbidden; indeed, they are liars. And they say, "There is none but our worldly life, and we will not be resurrected." If only you could see when they are made to stand before their Lord. He will say, "Is this not the truth?" They will say, "Yes, by our Lord." He will [then] say, "So taste the punishment for what you used to deny."
>
> [Qur’an, al-Anʿām 6:28–30]

We will expand on this topic later, God willing.

The transition from intellectual īmān to experiential, heartfelt īmān is the second of the stations of wayfaring to God in the religion of God — Mighty and Majestic is He. Many people remain with their īmān within the limits of outward deeds and outward words. Consider this authentic hadith:

> There will appear at the end of time people who are young in years but foolish in intellect; they will speak the best words of creation but their faith will not pass beyond their throats. They will pass through Islam as an arrow passes through its game; so wherever you find them, kill them, for indeed in killing them there is a reward on the Day of Resurrection for whoever kills them.
>
> [al-Bukhārī, Kitāb al-Īmān, Hadith 3344; Muslim, Kitāb al-Zakāh, Hadith 1066]

Here is a phenomenon described by the hadith: “Their faith does not pass beyond their throats.” It does not move from the throat to the heart; that is, speech does not reach the inner heart. It is a diseased condition indicating that a person has ceased wayfaring in the religion of God and has stopped at the first stage. If a person is able to pass beyond this stage, then his īmān reaches his heart; this īmān increases and increases until it becomes a consciousness of the attributes of God — Mighty and Majestic is He. Then the person reaches the station of iḥsān, which the Messenger described with his words: “That you worship God as though you see Him, for if you do not see Him, then indeed He sees you.”

This station of iḥsān is the pinnacle of īmān. When īmān is realized in the heart, it becomes iḥsān. Hence the hadith: “The best of īmān is that you know that God is witness over you wherever you are.” Between the two hadiths we understand that iḥsān is the best of īmān. From the definition of iḥsān in the hadith we understand that iḥsān is worshipping God in a specific conscious state. Worship in general, in the religion of God, leads to a station in the religion of God that is more refined: the station of taqwā. God — Exalted is He — says:

> O mankind, worship your Lord, who created you and those before you, that you may become righteous.
>
> [Qur’an, al-Baqarah 2:21]

Taqwā is the stage of complete maturation that interacts with Islam, īmān, and iḥsān. It is knowledge and action; it is a state of the heart and a way of conduct; it is a condition in which the intellect, the heart, and the limbs are in harmony. In the end it is God’s gift to whoever submits, acts, and excels. God — Exalted is He — says:

> And those who are guided — He increases them in guidance and gives them their righteousness.
>
> [Qur’an, Muḥammad 47:17]

So taqwā is God’s gift to one who is guided, and the beginning of guidance is īmān in God. God — Exalted is He — says:

> And whoever believes in Allah — He will guide his heart.
>
> [Qur’an, at-Taghābun 64:11]

The path to it is spiritual struggle. God — Exalted is He — says:

> And those who strive for Us — We will surely guide them to Our ways.
>
> [Qur’an, al-ʿAnkabūt 29:69]

Īmān in God accompanied by struggle of the soul through the performance of worship and the deeds of Islam leads to taqwā, which is īmān and following the Book. This is mentioned at the beginning of Sūrat al-Baqarah, a topic we discussed at length in the book *Jund Allāh Thaqāfatan wa-Akhlāqan*.

When a person realizes taqwā, taqwā conveys him to the station of gratitude, which is the highest of the stations of wayfaring in the religion of God — Exalted is He. Our evidence that taqwā leads to gratitude is God’s saying:

> Fear Allah that you may be grateful.
>
> [Qur’an, Āl ʿImrān 3:123]

Gratitude is the pinnacle of the stations, and few are its people. It is the station of the Messengers — peace and blessings be upon them. The Messenger of God said, when asked to perform a miracle: “Then I would be a most grateful servant.” And God — Exalted is He — says:

> Work, O family of David, in gratitude. And few of My servants are grateful.
>
> [Qur’an, Saba’ 34:13]

For a person to act in gratitude to God for His blessings — by dedicating everything God has given him to the path most beloved to God, in the light of God’s law, without neglecting any command of God, abandoning forbidden and disliked matters, and performing the obligatory, necessary, and recommended duties — in a heart-state that is the state of gratitude to God — Mighty and Majestic is He — that is the pinnacle of wayfaring in the religion of God.

Once all these meanings become clear, it becomes possible to recognize the group of errors people fall into in this chapter. There are people who imagine that it is enough for them to pray, fast, believe, and worship, without having a general conception of the religion of God, and without reaching taqwā in its broad sense — namely, absolute commitment to the law of God in personal and public affairs, and in realizing Islam in the self and on earth. Thus, although they profess taqwā, they do not know its true meaning, and they may imagine that the lowest of stations are beneath iḥsān for them. Consequently their conception of the station of gratitude becomes erroneous, and their realization is weak or deficient.

There are also people who build their conception on a deficient understanding of a noble hadith, isolating it from other texts, and imagining that everything has been gathered in it, whereas it is an explanation of some meanings and a clarification of the deficiency of others. It has its place within the whole of the religion of God; it is not to be understood in isolation from the texts. This is the famous hadith in which the Messenger of God — peace and blessings be upon him — spoke about Islam, īmān, and iḥsān, a topic we expanded upon in the introduction to our book on Islam; let it be reviewed there. The hadith clarifies the essence of the pillars of Islam with respect to the totality of Islam, explains what enters into the word īmān, and gives us a precise concept of iḥsān in the religion of God. Thus it is an elucidation of the religion of God insofar as it details important matters in the religion of God, but it does not mean that this alone is the religion of God.

## Obligation

Just as many people have fallen into errors concerning what has been mentioned, they have also fallen into errors concerning the issue of *taklīf* (تكليف): what it is, who the *mukallaf* (مكلف) is, and the types of obligations.

1. Among visible creatures, God — Mighty and Majestic is He — has charged the human being; and among creatures hidden from us, He has charged the *jinn*. God — Exalted is He — says:

> And I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me.
>
> [Qur’an, adh-Dhāriyāt 51:56]

So what is taklīf? Who is the mukallaf? What are the obligations?

As for taklīf, it has two definitions: the first is “the imposition of something involving difficulty”; the second is “the request for something involving difficulty.” The difference between the two definitions is that the first alludes to taklīf regarding the performance of obligations and the abandonment of forbidden things, while the second includes the performance of recommended acts and the abandonment of prohibited things. From these definitions, and from the essence of taklīf, we understand that what God — Mighty and Majestic is He — has charged His servants with involves some hardship. Therefore those who imagine that religion is only for comfort in the ordinary sense are mistaken.

As for the mukallaf, it is the adult, sane human being of sound senses to whom the call of the Messengers — peace and blessings be upon them — has reached; likewise the sane *jinnī* to whom the call of the Messengers has reached and who is of sound senses. Our scholars have said that the jinn are charged from the moment of their creation, so their charge is not contingent upon reaching puberty.

As for the obligations, some are intellectual, some are conceptual, some are verbal, and some are practical. The one who imposes them is God — Mighty and Majestic is He — through the Messengers — peace and blessings be upon them. The human being was not created by God in vain; rather He created him to charge him. God — Mighty and Majestic is He — did not create this universe without wisdom; rather He created it for a wisdom that cannot be realized without the existence of taklīf.

2. The first of obligations is knowledge of God — Mighty and Majestic is He; then knowledge of the Messengers; then knowledge of the law of God — Mighty and Majestic is He; then knowledge of what is incumbent upon every mukallaf from this law in detail; then knowledge of what is necessary to realize these obligations, for whatever is indispensable to an obligation is itself an obligation; and the commitment to everything that requires action, whether in learning or in application. In this matter many errors occur. For example, the correct general conception of the law of God is a duty neglected by many. The totality of what is demanded of every person in terms of knowledge and action is a matter many do not know; they treat it in a deficient, truncated manner. Awareness of the necessary consequences of performing many prescribed duties is absent from many people, and as a result they neglect duties. Therefore it is among the duties of this age to provide a comprehensive exposition of these matters.

3. Knowledge of God includes knowledge of His attributes, names, and acts; what is obligatory for Him; what is impossible in His regard; and what is permissible for Him. This is a vast subject in which most of creation have fallen into many errors, and God has protected *Ahl as-Sunnah wa-l-Jamāʿah* (أهل السنة والجماعة) in it. God — Exalted is He — says:

> Exalted is Allah above what they associate with Him, except the servants of Allah, sincere.
>
> [Qur’an, as-Sāffāt 37:159–160]

The sincere servants of God are those who describe God — Mighty and Majestic is He — in the manner due to Him.

Knowledge of the Messenger includes knowledge of what is obligatory in his regard, what is impossible, and what is permissible, and knowledge of a number of matters in this station. Knowledge of the Messenger’s law requires that a person have a general conception of this law: its principles, branches, self-evident matters, and landmarks. Among the matters incumbent upon every mukallaf in terms of knowledge specific to him is that a person know what is obligatory upon him of the stations of Islam, īmān, iḥsān, taqwā, and gratitude; this differs from one person to another in completeness and comprehensiveness. It is also incumbent upon every mukallaf to know the path to realizing obligations: the path to performing every duty and establishing it, whether it is an individual duty (*farḍ ʿayn*, فرض عين) or a communal duty (*farḍ kifāyah*, فرض كفاية). Among the foremost of these in our age is that a person know the path to making the word of God supreme in his country, in all the countries of the Muslim nation, and among people in general.

All of this is the general basis of action. There are duties in the realm of knowledge and duties in the realm of action. There are obligations God — Mighty and Majestic is He — has charged every individual with; but there are also obligations He has charged the whole nation with. Our scholars have designated all of this by the expression “individual duties” and “communal duties.” Many people err in this matter; many look at the topic of communal duties with a deficient view, and this deficient view causes these duties to be suspended.

4. As for communal duties: for example, it is well known that a communal duty remains an individual duty until it is performed — and sometimes a specific person or group is designated to perform a communal duty; then that communal duty becomes an individual duty for them. Often the comprehensive view of communal duties is absent from some people, and as a result the orientation toward them is absent, so the Muslim nation remains in a state of deficiency, backwardness, or delay. Often knowledge of the path to realizing access to communal duties is absent from some people, just as knowledge of the path to realizing access to individual duties may be absent from them, and there is much in that.

We have seen in this chapter that the mukallaf is the adult, sane human being of sound senses to whom the call has reached. Therefore the adult is the one charged. But what is the stage that qualifies him for the stage after puberty? What are the matters that should be given to every person before puberty? And which Muslims are attentive to them and give them their due? These too are among the important matters in which many fall into errors or deficient and weak conceptions. The reason for all of this is the loss of correct learning and the lack of a person’s comprehensive grasp of the message of God — Mighty and Majestic is He — except for the few whom God has honored.

## The Soul, the Spirit, the Heart, and the Intellect

Just as many people have fallen into errors concerning what has been mentioned, they have also fallen into errors concerning their view of things within themselves or from themselves. For example, a person knows that he has an intellect, and he speaks of something called the heart, something called the spirit (*rūḥ*, روح), something called the soul (*nafs*, نفس), and something called life. These matters are among the closest things to a human being; yet in this station one finds errors that are almost innumerable: errors among non-Muslims and errors among Muslims. It is not surprising that the disbeliever should fail to have a correct perception of these secrets; but it is surprising that a Muslim, who ought to have the correct answer, should not have these meanings clear to him.

Hence we find confusion among many regarding the conception of the *sharʿī* intellect, the intellect that is the instrument of thought, and the organ of thought that is the brain; and regarding the heart, which is something else located in the breast. We find confusion between speaking of the physical heart and the other heart, and a lack of clarity in the conceptions of the soul and spirit: when the soul is identical with the spirit, when the soul and spirit are identical with the heart and the intellect, and when the matter is otherwise. Then there is life and its connection to these things: the life of the sperm cell; the life of the fetus before the spirit is breathed into it; and the life of the fetus after the spirit is breathed into it. There are many errors concerning these matters, some small and some of no consequence; but in any case it is appropriate for us to say a word about this topic, and this word is important for the clarity of this epistle. This epistle will clarify some of these matters gradually.

Many people confuse the issue of the intellect, the heart, the spirit, and the soul in Islamic terminology, and consequently fall into successive errors. Often Islamic writers enter into discussions and disputes as a result of ambiguity in this matter. The reason for this — and God knows best — is that the Lawgiver has given these matters technical terms, while people use them in other senses; hence confusion arises in this matter, and this confusion sometimes leads to disbelief or denial of something known necessarily from the religion. Let us give clear examples:

### The Heart

The word “heart” is applied to the physical heart whose place is the breast, and the Lawgiver also uses the word “heart” for another heart whose place is the breast, connected to the physical heart, and which is the locus of īmān and disbelief. It is the habit of poets and writers to speak of the heart as the locus of emotions such as love and hate, and there is no doubt that a connection exists between the heart in the speech of poets and writers and the heart that is the locus of disbelief, hypocrisy, and īmān. And there is no doubt that the physical heart is one thing, and this other heart is another thing. Do you not see, for example, in our age, where a physical heart has been replaced by a living heart, that emotions do not change as a result? Once you grasp this meaning, you know the difference between the heart in the Lawgiver’s terminology and the heart in people’s terminology; confusion about this causes many errors. Just as this occurs with the heart, it occurs with the spirit, the soul, and the intellect, and has led to errors connected with doctrines. Therefore our scholars considered discussion of this topic part of the inquiries into doctrines, and it is likewise a principal part of the science of taṣawwuf; indeed, it is its core. Our scholars discussed it in books of doctrine because there is an unseen aspect to these matters, and detailed treatment of unseen matters belongs to the Lawgiver. The Lawgiver alone tells us about them, and our stance toward them is īmān and submission. However, although these matters are unseen, their existence is perceived, and their possessor can sense them, while others can perceive their effects. Therefore they are unseen from one angle and perceived from another; human experience and sensation play a large role in recognizing them. Hence this topic is intertwined: matters of doctrine intertwine with matters of taṣawwuf and with matters of rational knowledge and experience. Thus every group of people has conceptions of these matters that differ from those of other groups, and every group has claims in this regard.

Sound, sound knowledge alone places things in their proper places in these matters, because it is illumined by its Lord, and its Lord has guided it to the practical paths that lead it to knowledge of every matter by its appropriate route: what is reached by experience is reached by experience, what is reached by reason is reached by reason, and what is reached by the Lawgiver’s explanation is reached by His explanation.

We should not fail to record here that the matters of Islamic doctrine are not separated from the issues of realization, taste, and sulūk; and that speaking of them in an abstract manner must be followed by speaking of them elsewhere. Hence we find discussion of the heart, spirit, and soul distributed between books of doctrine and books of taṣawwuf. Because taṣawwuf has suffered what it has suffered, and because the scholars of doctrine have become so technical that ordinary people find their questions difficult, many meanings have been hidden from the common Muslim. Here we are on the path of clarifying the general conception of the soul, spirit, heart, and intellect. We begin with what *Ḥujjat al-Islām* al-Ghazālī said in his *Iḥyāʾ*:

He said under the heading “Clarification of the Meaning of the Soul, the Spirit, the Heart, and the Intellect, and What Is Intended by These Names”:

> Know that these four names are used in these chapters. Few of the great scholars have encompassed these names, the differences in their meanings, their limits, and their applications. Most errors originate in ignorance of the meaning of these names and their shared application to different referents. We explain here what is relevant to our purpose.
>
> **The first term: “heart.”** It has two meanings. One of them: the conical piece of flesh placed in the left side of the breast; it is a specific kind of flesh, and inside it is a cavity in which there is blood. It is the source of the spirit and its stomach. We do not now intend to describe its shape and manner, for that is the concern of physicians and is not related to religious purposes. This heart exists in beasts. When we use the word “heart” in this book, we do not mean that, for it is a piece of flesh of no significance, belonging to the world of visible and witnessed things, since beasts perceive it by sight, let alone human beings.
>
> **The second meaning** is a subtle, divine, spiritual faculty connected to this physical heart. That subtle faculty is the true human being, the universal knower and perceiver in man; it is the one addressed, reproached, punished, and required. Its connection to the physical heart has puzzled the minds of most people, for its attachment to it resembles the attachment of accidents to bodies, of attributes to described things, of a user to a tool, or of what resides to its place. We refrain from explaining this for two reasons: one, because it is connected to the sciences of unveiling, and our purpose in this book is only the sciences of practical dealing; two, because realizing it requires revealing the secret of the spirit, and since the Messenger of God — peace and blessings be upon him — did not speak about it, no one else should speak about it. Our intention is that when we use the word “heart” in this book, we mean this subtle faculty, and our purpose is to mention its attributes and states, not to mention its reality in itself. The science of practical dealing requires knowledge of its attributes and states, not knowledge of its reality.
>
> **The second term: “spirit.”** It too is used in two senses relevant to our subject. One of them: a subtle body whose source is the cavity of the physical heart, spreading through the pulsating veins to all parts of the body. Its flow in the body and the flooding of the lights of life, sense, sight, hearing, and smell through its organs resembles the flooding of light from a lamp that is carried around the corners of a house, for it reaches no part of the house without illumining it. Life is like the light produced in the walls, and the spirit is like the lamp; the flowing and movement of the spirit inside is like the movement of the lamp around the corners of the house as its handle is turned. When physicians use the word “spirit,” they mean this sense: a subtle vapor refined by the heat of the heart. Explaining it is not our concern, for it is the concern of the physicians who treat bodies. As for the concern of the physicians of religion who treat the heart until it is led to the vicinity of the Lord of the Worlds, it is not related to the explanation of this spirit at all.
>
> **The second meaning** is the subtle, knowing, perceiving faculty in man, which we explained as one of the meanings of the heart, and which God — Exalted is He — intended by His saying:
>
> > Say, "The Spirit is from the command of my Lord."
> >
> > [Qur’an, al-Isrā’ 17:85]
>
> It is the light of the mercy that transcends speech and words from comprehending its reality.
>
> **The third term: “soul.”** It too is shared between several meanings; two of them are relevant to our purpose. One is that it means the greedy urge that combines the powers of anger and appetite in man, as will be explained later. This usage is dominant among the people of taṣawwuf, because by “soul” they mean the source that combines the blameworthy traits of man; they say: one must strive against the soul and break it. Alluded to in this is the Prophet’s saying — peace and blessings be upon him — “Your worst enemy is your soul that is between your sides.”
>
> **The second meaning** is the subtle faculty we mentioned, which is the true human being; it is the soul of man and his essence. However, it is described by different attributes according to the variation of its states. If it is tranquil under command, and agitation leaves it because of the cessation of opposition by appetites, it is called the tranquil soul. God — Exalted is He — said concerning it:
>
> > O reassured soul, return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing [to Him].
> >
> > [Qur’an, al-Fajr 89:27–28]
>
> The soul in the first sense cannot be conceived as returning to God — Exalted is He — for it is far from God and belongs to the party of Satan. If it has not yet attained tranquility but has become opposed to the appetitive soul and remonstrates with it, it is called the reproaching soul, for it reproaches its possessor when he falls short in worshipping his Lord. God — Exalted is He — said:
>
> > And I swear by the reproaching soul.
> >
> > [Qur’an, al-Qiyāmah 75:2]
>
> If it ceases remonstration, submits, and obeys the demands of appetites and the incitements of Satan, it is called the soul that commands evil. God — Exalted is He — narrated from the wife of al-ʿAzīz:
>
> > And I do not absolve myself. Indeed, the soul is a persistent enjoiner of evil.
> >
> > [Qur’an, Yūsuf 12:53]
>
> It is possible to say that “the enjoiner of evil” refers to the soul in the first sense. Thus the soul in the first sense is blameworthy in the extreme, while in the second sense it is something whose non-existence is inconceivable: it is the soul of man, that is, his essence and reality, which knows God — Exalted is He — and all knowable things.
>
> **The fourth term: “intellect.”** It too is shared between different meanings, which we mentioned in the book *al-ʿIlm*. Two of them are relevant to our purpose. One: it may be used to mean knowledge of the realities of things; then it is an expression for the attribute of knowledge that belongs to the heart. The second: it may be used to mean the knowing faculty by which the sciences are known; then it is the heart, that is, that subtle faculty. We know that every knower has in himself an existence that subsists by itself; knowledge is an attribute that inheres in it, and an attribute is not the thing described. “Intellect” may be used to mean the attribute of knowledge, and it may be used to mean the locus of perception, that is, the perceiver.
>
> So it has become clear to you that the meanings of these matters exist and are: the physical heart, the physical spirit, the physical appetitive soul, and the sciences. These are four meanings to which the four terms are applied. There is a fifth meaning: the subtle, knowing, perceiving faculty in man, to which all four terms are collectively applied. Thus the meanings are five and the terms are four, and each term is used for two meanings. Most scholars have been confused by the differences and overlapping of these terms, so you see them saying in their thoughts: this is a thought of the intellect, this a thought of the spirit, this a thought of the heart, this a thought of the soul — while the observer does not know the difference in meaning of these names. In order to lift the veil on this, we have presented this explanation of these names. Whenever the word “heart” occurs in the Qur’an and the Sunnah, what is intended is the meaning that understands from man and knows the reality of things; it may be referred to as the heart in the breast because there is a special connection between that subtle faculty and the body of the heart. Although it is connected to the whole body and is used for it, it is connected to it through the heart; its first connection is to the heart, as though the heart were its mount, tool, world, and conveyance.
>
> (End of quotation.)

From al-Ghazālī’s words we understand that the soul, intellect, heart, and spirit sometimes have one meaning, although the names differ according to the attribute of the human spirit. If appetite overcomes this spirit, it is called “soul”; if the spirit overcomes forbidden appetite, it is called “intellect”; if its īmān states become manifest, it is called “heart”; and if it knows God with true knowledge and renders Him pure servitude, it is called “spirit.” But these things sometimes refer to something other than what we mentioned: “soul” may mean blood, or life; people sometimes apply “intellect” to the place of thought, which is the brain, sometimes to intelligence, and sometimes to the organizing principle of the body — all of which are connected to the brain. They may mention the spirit and mean by it mere life. Then what is this life? People differ in answering. As a result of all this, a number of errors and confusions occur in these stations.

For example, a disbeliever may come to a text indicating one meaning in these matters and impose another meaning upon it in order to confuse the ignorant. And we find some Muslims overcome by one who makes observations in these matters, so they load all these meanings upon them in all circumstances, without a clear distinction. The human being begins as a sperm cell, and each cell has its own life. When the sperm cell unites with the ovum, a living piece connected to the life of the mother’s body is found; when it reaches such-and-such months, the spirit enters it, and its own movement begins. Cellular life exists before the existence of the spirit; it does not contradict or conflict with it. A disbeliever may deliberately confuse the issue of the spirit and life. Some of them did so when they came to the words of God — Exalted is He —:

> How do you disbelieve in Allah when you were lifeless and He brought you to life?
>
> [Qur’an, al-Baqarah 2:28]

They said: this text indicates that the sperm cell is dead while it is alive. But the text means the state of the parts of the sperm cell before it was created. Its parts are nothing but dead atoms that became nourishment, and from them the sperm cell came into being. Thus the journey of human life began… Therefore cellular life is one thing, and the arrival of the spirit after that is another; they do not contradict each other, but rather are two complementary things.

Consider now the state of madness and the state called by the Sufis “attraction” (*jadh*b). Madness is sometimes a state connected to the brain, while attraction is a state connected to the heart. The brain is connected to what people call the intellect, and the heart is connected to what people call the intellect; the *sharʿī* intellect is connected to the brain from one angle and to the heart from another. Hence the scholars said: the intellect is the heart, and many texts testify to that. By this is meant here the *sharʿī* intellect by which a person regulates his conduct according to the law of God.

Notice that a kind of medicine calms the nerves, so we find that a person who takes it is not agitated; notice also that a kind of medicine puts a person in a state of complete arousal. Thus we find that what is introduced into the blood affects the person’s state in general. Therefore blood can in some states be the soul; the word “soul” may be applied to the whole self, or to the appetitive and nervous behavior of a person. People err in these stations, calling one thing by the name of another, while the aspect is different. We are not here attempting a detailed exposition, but we want to clarify one of the points in which error occurs; and we think the matter has become somewhat clear. Let us summarize this topic as follows:

There is life for the fetus before the spirit descends into it; there is a soul for the human being that is an effect of a group of physiological and environmental factors in the body after the spirit has come into it; there is a brain that regulates the whole matter of the body and to which the spirit is connected; and there is a physical heart to which the spirit is connected. The fetus in its mother’s womb, before the spirit descends into it, derives its life from its mother’s life; but after the spirit descends into it, it has its complete, independent life, in a kind of independence. Therefore when this spirit is withdrawn from the human being, he dies thereafter. By this we understand the difference between the life of the fetus without spirit while in its mother’s womb before the spirit is breathed into it, and its death thereafter when the spirit is withdrawn.

When the spirit descends into the body, it is affected by the various physiological and bodily factors. The factors of appetite and anger affect it. It may overcome these by sulūk along the connecting path, or appetite and anger may overcome it. Here is the arena of conflict between the guidance of the prophets, which seeks to keep the spirit in its sound nature, and the temptation of the devils among mankind and the jinn, which seeks to make the spirit follow desires.

The jurists call blood “soul”; for example, they say: if an animal that has no flowing soul dies and falls into water… by which they mean blood. The author of *al-Muntaqā* titled one of the chapters: “Chapter on what has no flowing soul so that it does not become impure by death.”

Consider now this medical statement by Dr. Khāliṣ Kanjū concerning the physical inclination: the secret lies in the internal ovulation process, where a small follicle carrying the ovum bursts in order to unite with it, from the ovary to the fallopian tube, where fertilization occurs in the final outer third of it; the latter phenomenon requires attention. Hormones flow from this small sac into the body in abundance, raising physical tension in the woman; and this in turn returns to the hidden cycle of mutual exchange between the soul and the body.

Thus blood and its components have a great connection with the spirit and an effect upon it. There is a weak hadith from the Messenger of God in which he said: “Anger is a coal in the heart of the son of Adam.” Therefore the things present in the blood have a connection to the issue of appetite and anger. Hence the bodily constitution has an effect on the spirit, and this effect is strengthened or weakened; the person may surrender to this effect, resist it, or seek to control it. It is important that there is a connection between the body, its composition, its purposes, the protection of the spirit, and that each has its effect on the other. The Messengers — peace and blessings be upon them — are the ones who showed us the limits of interaction between body and spirit, or between the appetitive soul and the spirit.

## Ijtihād and Taqlīd

Errors also occurred concerning the issues of *taqlīd* (تقليد) and *ijtihād* (اجتهاد): what a person cannot be excused for ignoring, what he may be excused for ignoring, in what he may follow an authority, in what he may not follow an authority, what he must reject outright because it contradicts what is necessarily known of the religion, and what may be subject to research and investigation. To grasp part of this matter, we say:

Our scholars distinguish between taqlīd in the foundations of the *Sharīʿah* (الشريعة) and taqlīd in its branches, between taqlīd in clear, self-evident matters and taqlīd in doubtful matters. These are matters that few people place in their proper positions or know the limits of their issues. Ignorance of them has become widespread even among those who present themselves for knowledge and teaching. Hence affliction has spread and matters have become unclear to many people.

The original rule is that taqlīd in the foundations of religion, that is, in doctrines, is not permissible. The original rule is that taqlīd in everything necessarily known of the religion is not permissible; the scholars differ as to whether the limit of impermissibility reaches disbelief or only sinful disobedience. The original rule among them is that the non-scholar may follow an authority in the branches of the Sharīʿah that the ordinary person is unable to know God’s ruling in by himself — namely, the *mujtahid* imams — but the limits of these meanings are broad. Which are the doctrines in which taqlīd is not permissible? What are the self-evident matters of the Sharīʿah that a Muslim cannot be excused for not knowing? What are the branches that a Muslim may be excused for ignoring and in which he may follow an authority? Often there is deficiency in expressing these things.

Knowledge of God, the path to knowing the Messengers of God — peace and blessings be upon them — the evidences that point to God and His attributes, and the evidences that establish that Muḥammad is the Messenger of God: all of these are foundations. Knowledge of the foundations of the Islamic Sharīʿah — what the Book, the Sunnah, and consensus (*ijmāʿ*, إجماع) are, and what the Book, the Sunnah, and consensus have established of criteria and agreed-upon standards — all of this is from the foundations. Whatever is clear in the Book, the Sunnah, and consensus, if there is verbal or conceptual *tawātur* (تواتر), is from the foundations. The whole Qur’an is verbally *mutawātir* (متواتر), and many texts of the Sunnah are verbally or conceptually mutawātir. All of this, if its meaning is clear and decisively indicative, is from what is necessarily known of the religion; a Muslim cannot be excused for ignorance of it, and taqlīd in it is of no avail.

However, there is a difference between taqlīd in some kinds of doctrines and taqlīd in others, and between taqlīd in some foundations and taqlīd in branches. There are issues in which taqlīd of the Lawgiver alone is obligatory; there are issues in which rational conviction combined with scriptural evidence is obligatory; in the branches, following the Ummah is obligatory for the non-*mujtahid*, together with knowledge of the evidence if the person is a scholar; and for the layperson, following the Ummah is obligatory, without being required to know the evidence. These too are among the subtle matters of this station.

The foundations and self-evident matters of the Sharīʿah include many things: knowledge of God, knowledge of the spiritual way to Him, knowledge of the Messenger, knowledge of the necessity of following the Book and the Sunnah, knowledge of obligations and prohibitions, knowledge of some matters established by tawātur. Among the important matters included here are knowledge of the obligation of purifying the soul, matters of heartfelt and intellectual īmān, the general conception of Islam, the obligation of *jihād* to make the word of God supreme, the obligation of what God has revealed, the obligation of knowing that the Muslim Ummah is one Ummah and that political unity is obligatory, and many matters that cannot be enumerated. This book is an exposition of some of these matters and placing them in their proper places.

The matters in which taqlīd of the Lawgiver alone is permissible, and the matters in which a person must reach rational conviction, do not require that the person be able to enumerate them or mention details about them. It is enough that if asked about them, he does not deny them, and that he knows some of the summary evidences concerning them. Once you recognize the limits of taqlīd, you will find much room for error in this matter: a person who follows an authority where taqlīd is not permissible; a person who hesitates to follow an authority where it is permissible; and a person whom enthusiasm drives to follow errors attributed to someone, which may be falsely ascribed to him. All of this a Muslim must free himself from.

Thus we find errors in the general conception of Islam, errors in the conception of the issue of īmān, errors in the general conception of the stations of wayfaring in the religion of God, errors in the issue of taklīf, and errors in the conceptions of the soul, intellect, heart, and spirit. All of these have negative repercussions upon the Muslim and upon Islamic life. If we search for the cause of the many errors we have mentioned, we find that it returns to the loss of correct, comprehensive knowledge, especially among the scholars from whom seekers take concepts and conceptions, and who are the practical example and reference. The comprehensive, total view of Islam is sometimes missing; the correct, comprehensive understanding of the Book and the Sunnah is deficient; the general conception of the methods of deriving legal rulings is weak; the sciences that have branched from the Book and the Sunnah — fiqh, *tawḥīd* (توحيد), and taṣawwuf — have conceptions that are either deficient, weak, non-comprehensive, or erroneous; and what is required from other aspects, all of which are necessary for completing an integrated Islamic culture, is found shaky or nonexistent. The righteous example in all of this, and the wholesome environments for conveying all of this, are almost confined.

For some of these reasons we wrote the book *Jund Allāh Thaqāfatan wa-Akhlāqan*, the book *Jawlatān fī al-Fiqhayn al-Kabīr wa-l-Akbar wa-Uṣūlihimā*, and this series; for taṣawwuf and its circles were among the chief means through which error infiltrated many circles.

It is fitting that the seekers of truth, when we mention deficiencies and distribute blame here and there, should know that we do not intend to disparage any of them; rather, we want the misunderstanding of our brothers, the students of knowledge, to be removed so that we all may attain what is due to us. Therefore I have detailed this introduction in these matters so that no one will forget that, despite the breadth of what this book contains of what a person needs, this book is only a correction of some matters in one aspect. All of this is to point out that this book is a part of a whole. Let us now begin to speak about the science of taṣawwuf.
