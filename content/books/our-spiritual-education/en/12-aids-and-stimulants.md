# Chapter 12: Aids and Stimulants of the Journey

It is observed that people’s turning toward Allah increases in some states, and that the *sālik* (سالك) to Allah passes through periods of laziness. There are stimulants that increase a person’s turning toward Allah, or renew his resolve when it slackens. Among them are: gathering for knowledge, or for Qur’an recitation, or for *dhikr* (ذكر), or for mutual study; and there is *inshād* (إنشاد); and there is reading in the books of the *sayr* (سير) to Allah and the stories of the righteous. Some of these stimulants may fulfill an obligation, and at the same time be a stimulant for the *sayr* or a renewer of resolve, such as gathering for a prescribed prayer. In the issues of gathering, or the issues of *inshād*, or the issues of reading in the books of the *sayr* to Allah and the stories of the righteous, there are matters that must be observed, and there are errors that must be heeded.

Before we begin presenting the stimulants of the *sayr*, we would like to point out two things:

**First:** The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said:

> For every worker there is a period of zeal, and for every period of zeal there is a period of lassitude. So if he draws near and comes close, then look to him; and if he points away with his fingers, then do not count him. [Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhi, Book of Knowledge, Hadith 2391]

The *sāʾir* (سائر) to Allah must observe himself constantly, and he must manage his soul well. If he finds in himself a period of lassitude, let him try to preserve a minimum of work. Among the most important means by which the wayfarer manages himself in a period of lassitude is benefiting from the stimulants of the *sayr* that we shall mention. Thus the stimulants of the *sayr* are part of managing the soul in the matter of the *sayr* to Allah, but they are not the whole of that management.

**Second:** Every heart has a certain capacity to bear little works. If the heart is burdened beyond its capacity, a relapse occurs. Likewise the *nafs* (نفس): if it is burdened beyond its capacity, or not given its necessary needs, or some of its permissible demands, then it overcomes the person at that point. Therefore we must always attend to this matter—whether we are taking or giving.

In the hadith:

> Take from works what you can bear, for by Allah, Allah does not weary until you weary. *Sharah* means activity; *fatrah* means laziness. [Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhi; also narrated by Mālik, Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim]

In another hadith:

> Straighten [your aims] and come close, and know that none of you will enter Paradise by his deeds. They said: “Not even you, O Messenger of Allah?” He said: “Not even I, unless Allah envelopes me in forgiveness and mercy.” [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Faith, Hadith 99]

We must observe, in the matter of stimulants of the *sayr*, that they do not turn into the opposite of what is intended when they are made too heavy for the soul, or when some of them go beyond what was prescribed for them. We must know the wisdom in each of them fundamentally. And since the issue of gathering in particular entails many benefits, we shall speak about it with moderate detail, beginning with:

## 1. Gathering

Gathering in Islam has a great importance, because of the good effects that follow from it. Indeed, it is necessary in many situations for establishing obligations, or duties, or recommended acts, or for acquiring kinds of good. There are the congregational prayers—especially the Friday prayer and the two ʿEid prayers—and there is gathering for a matter that unites the concerns of the Muslims, and there is gathering for knowledge, or *dhikr*, or mutual study.

Included in gathering for knowledge is gathering for the Qur’an or the *Sunnah* (سنة), or the sciences of the Book and the *Sunnah*, or gathering for the Arabic language, or gathering for *fiqh* (فقه) and *tawḥīd* (توحيد), or purification of the soul, or *uṣūl al-fiqh* (أصول الفقه), or the Sīrah and Islamic history, or modern Islamic studies, or studies of the conspiracy against Islam, or studies of the *fiqh* of *daʿwah* (دعوة). All of this enters into gathering for knowledge, whether it takes the form of a gathering in a public or private circle, regular or occasional.

The basis for that is the words of the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, in the hadith narrated by Imam Muslim:

> No people gather in a house of the houses of Allah, reciting the Book of Allah and studying it among themselves, except that tranquility descends upon them, mercy envelops them, angels surround them, and Allah mentions them among those near Him. [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Mosques, Hadith 1533]

Notice what results from gathering for the Book of Allah: descent of tranquility, envelopment of mercy, angels surrounding, and Allah, Mighty and Majestic, mentioning its people. And what good results from that! For example, the envelopment of mercy results in affection and gathering of hearts. Allah, the Exalted, said:

> And if your Lord had willed, He could have made mankind one community; but they will not cease to differ. Except whom your Lord has given mercy, and for that He created them. But the word of your Lord is to be fulfilled that, “I will surely fill Hell with jinn and men all together.” [Qur’an, Hūd 11:118-119]

So the merciful are those who do not differ. And from the ways of receiving Allah’s mercy is gathering on His Book. And the descent of tranquility results in increase of faith. Allah, the Exalted, said:

> It is He who sent down tranquillity into the hearts of the believers that they would increase in faith along with their [present] faith. [Qur’an, Al-Fatḥ 48:4]

Gathering for what we have mentioned has a direct connection to the Qur’an, or an indirect connection to serving the Qur’an, or to realizing the goals of the Qur’an, or to realizing what protects from distance from the Qur’an; and all of it enters into gathering for knowledge.

And in the hadith:

> If you pass by the gardens of Paradise, graze. They said: “And what are the gardens of Paradise?” He said: “The circles of *dhikr*.” [Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhi, Book of Supplications, Hadith 3510]

Some interpreted this hadith as referring to knowledge, and some as referring to *dhikr*. And in the second hadith there is an indication that joining a circle of knowledge is taking refuge with Allah, Mighty and Majestic: “The Messenger of Allah was sitting in the mosque when three men came; two of them came to the Messenger of Allah and stood over him. As for one of them, he saw a gap in the circle and sat in it; as for the other, he sat behind them; and as for the third, he turned away and left. When the Messenger of Allah finished, he said: ‘Shall I not tell you about the three men? As for one of them, he took refuge with Allah, so Allah gave him refuge; as for the other, he felt shy, so Allah felt shy of him; and as for the other, he turned away, so Allah turned away from him.’” [Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Book of Friday Prayer, Hadith 1304; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Mosques, Hadith 1528; Muwaṭṭaʾ Mālik; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhi]

Gathering for knowledge entails many benefits: the stirring of resolve, acquaintance with new wisdom, or remembrance of a matter that should be remembered. All of this, when the knowledge is sound, the intention sincere, and those who undertake it qualified for it, results in companionship, reception, and absorption of the wholesome heart-state; and all of that assists the *sayr* to Allah.

It was said: “A cure may be hoped from the disease, whatever is inseparable from it.” The wise shaykh knows how to arrange the matter of gathering for knowledge so as to direct every individual to what suits him of the *sayr* through public and private circles. He must observe the readiness of the *sālikīn* (سالكين), arrange sessions of exhortation, and observe in that the *Sunnah* and the life of the Companions.

Al-Bukhārī and Muslim narrated from Shaqīq, who said: “Ibn Masʿūd used to remind the people every Thursday, so a man said to him: ‘O Abā ʿAbd al-Raḥmān, I wish that you would remind us every day.’ He said: ‘It would indeed be too much. I dislike boring you, and I alternate sermons with you as the Messenger of Allah used to alternate them with us, for fear of boredom toward us.’” And al-Bukhārī narrated from ʿIkrimah that Ibn ʿAbbās said: “Address the people once on Friday; if you refuse, then twice; if you increase, then three times, and do not bore the people with this Qur’an. Do not let me find you coming to people while they are engaged in some *ḥadīth* and you narrate to them, cutting off their *ḥadīth* and boring them. Rather, be silent; if they ask you, then speak while they desire it. And avoid rhyming in supplication, for I saw the Messenger of Allah and his Companions not doing that.” [Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Book of Knowledge, Hadith 68]

There is also gathering for *dhikr*. Its basis is what the Two Shaykhs narrated from Abū Hurayrah from the Messenger of Allah, that he said:

> Allah has angels who roam the roads seeking out the people of *dhikr*. If they find a people remembering Allah, they call to one another: “Come to what you need!” And they surround them with their wings up to the lowest heaven. Then their Lord asks them—though He is most knowing of them: “What do My servants say?” They say: “They say: Subḥānaka (glory be to You), Allāhu akbar (Allah is greatest), al-ḥamdulillāh (praise be to Allah), and lā ilāha illā Allāh (there is no deity but Allah).” He says: “Have they seen Me?” They say: “No, by Allah, they have not seen You.” He says: “How would it be if they saw Me?” They say: “If they saw You, they would be more devoted to You, more intense in glorifying You, and more abundant in praising You.” He says: “What do they ask for?” They say: “They ask for Paradise.” He says: “Have they seen it?” They say: “No, by Allah, O Lord, they have not seen it.” He says: “How would it be if they saw it?” They say: “If they saw it, they would be more eager for it, more intense in seeking it, and greater in desire for it.” He says: “From what do they seek refuge?” They say: “From the Fire.” He says: “Have they seen it?” They say: “No, by Allah, they have not seen it.” He says: “How would it be if they saw it?” They say: “If they saw it, they would be more frantic in fleeing from it, and more intense in fear of it.” He says: “I make you witness that I have forgiven them.” One of the angels says: “Among them is so-and-so, who is not one of them; he only came for a need.” He says: “They are the people whose companions will not be wretched.” [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Remembrance, Hadith 2706; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Book of Supplication, Hadith 6408]

From this hadith we understand that the Messenger of Allah encouraged gathering for *dhikr*, and he explained to us the comprehensive basis upon which the circle of *dhikr* rests: glorification, declaration [of His oneness], magnification, praise, and supplication. So if a group gathers upon “Subḥān Allāh, al-ḥamdulillāh, lā ilāha illā Allāh, Allāhu akbar” and ends its session with supplication and seeking refuge, it has realized the *Sunnah* of gathering for *dhikr* as it occurs in the hadith.

Whoever disputes the *Sunnah* of that—i.e., its establishment in the *Sunnah*—contradicts the obvious understanding of this hadith. And if the *Sunnah* of gathering for *dhikr* is established in the meaning of this *ṣaḥīḥ* hadith, there are other texts that point to the like of this. Among them is what Muslim, al-Tirmidhi, and al-Nasāʾī narrated from Abū Saʿīd from Muʿāwiyah, may Allah be pleased with him, who said: “The Messenger of Allah came out upon a circle of his Companions and said: ‘What has gathered you?’ They said: ‘We have gathered to remember Allah and praise Him for guiding us to Islam and bestowing it upon us.’” [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Remembrance, Hadith 2704; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhi, Book of Supplications, Hadith 3518]

And among them is what al-Ṭabarānī narrated in *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr* with a *ḥasan* isnād from the Messenger of Allah, who said: “Allah will bring forth a people on the Day of Resurrection upon whose faces will be light, on pulpits of pearl; the people will envy them, though they are neither prophets nor martyrs.” An Arab said, falling to his knees: “O Messenger of Allah, describe them to us so that we may know them.” He said: “They are those who love one another for Allah’s sake, from various tribes and distant lands, gathered upon the *dhikr* of Allah.” [Al-Ṭabarānī, *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr*]

From such texts the *ṣūfiyyah* (صوفية) proceeded to insist on circles of *dhikr*. They extrapolated from these principles, then expanded in that expansively, and invented types of *adh-kār* (أذكار) by various methods, and organized for them various types of *dhikr* circles, until every shaykh had his own method, his own particular way in the *dhikr* in which his brethren gather. Some of them mixed *inshād* with *dhikr*, and they became ingenious in forms of sung *dhikr*: from sitting, to standing, to movements and gyrations. As a result, much disapproval occurred, many disputes, and long discussions, caused by not adhering to the clear evidential limits.

Shaykh al-Bannā, may Allah have mercy on him, made daily gathering for *dhikr* part of the etiquette of the Muslim. For that he made the major litany and summarized it as the minor litany. Although it is an established *Sunnah*, some of them disapproved of his making it a gathering; and that is ignorant disapproval.

For suppose a Companion were always with the Messenger of Allah and heard from him what he encouraged of morning and evening litanies; then this Companion committed himself to all of them, combining some with others—would he thereby be an innovator? Then if a group of Companions asked him to lead them in all of this, or requested from him supplication for that, would they be innovators after the Messenger of Allah had encouraged the basis of *dhikr* and the basis of gathering?

In any case, if the shaykh arranges a *dhikr* session once a week, or more than that, or a daily session according to the readiness and need of the *sālikīn*, there is much good in it. Its original evidence is from the words of the Messenger of Allah, especially as we live in an age in which matter has overwhelmed spirit, and the darkness of the heart has become great.

By the way, we say: Our stance toward the *dhikr* circles customary among some *ṣūfiyyah*, with all that is in them, is the stance of the jurists. It seems the jurists are not at ease with much of what has occurred in these circles, and their expressions differ in severity and leniency. We shall not allow ourselves to enter into battle with anyone over a juristic position he holds; but at the same time, we would like our point of departure in all our affairs to be the *Sunnah*. On this basis, we work to establish *dhikr* circles to which no jurist objects, and we call people to them, and we do not enter into battle with anyone over his juristic position, but we explain to him our viewpoint without entering with him into a debate that leads us with him to the condemned quarrel.

Many of the common people in our lands have been at ease with a kind of *dhikr* circles they call “gatherings for sending blessings upon the Messenger of Allah.” The name is taken from their being seated; each of them sends blessings upon the Messenger of Allah individually, then they read some Qur’an, then they remember Allah, Mighty and Majestic, with the formula “lā ilāha illā Allāh,” then it ends with supplication. Some do that on Friday morning, some at other times; some add to that; and some introduce into it matters that make it an object of disapproval. In all cases, we need *dhikr* circles acceptable in *fiqh* and knowledge, with clear evidence, proceeding on clear principles.

There is also gathering for mutual study between two or more people, in which they review what draws them near to Allah. Its basis is the hadith of Ibn Rawāḥah: whenever he met a man from the Companions of the Messenger of Allah, he would say, “Come, let us believe in our Lord for an hour.” Concerning that the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said: “May Allah have mercy on Ibn Rawāḥah, for he loves the gatherings that the angels boast about.” [Sunan Ibn Mājah, Book of Zuhd, Hadith 4194]

Mutual study may be between two brothers in Allah, or between the shaykh and a *sālik*. The whole Islamic gathering—whether it is a prayer gathering, or a sermon gathering, or a knowledge gathering, or a *dhikr* gathering, or a study gathering—stirs the person toward the *sayr* to Allah if the matter in it is sound. Therefore the Companions disliked that a person seclude himself from people to the desert or the like except in very special circumstances, because of the distance from good or hardness in character that results from it. In the hadith: “Whoever turns away becomes estranged.” [Sunan Abū Dāwūd, Book of Manners, Hadith 4913; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhi, Book of Zuhd, Hadith 2433]

The shaykh must observe, in arranging his gatherings, the condition of people, the states of the *sālikīn*, and the effect of that on their religious obligations and worldly concerns; and he must observe gentleness in the whole matter, for that is the etiquette of the Muslim.

> Allah is gentle and loves gentleness in all affairs. [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Virtues, Hadith 2165]

> Allah is gentle and loves gentleness, and gives through gentleness what He does not give through harshness, and what He does not give otherwise. [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Virtues, Hadith 2593]

> Gentleness adorns nothing without beautifying it, and removes nothing without disgracing it. [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Virtues, Hadith 2594]

> Whenever the Messenger of Allah was given a choice between two matters, he chose the easier of them, so long as it was not a sin. [Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Book of Softening Hearts, Hadith 6784; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Virtues, Hadith 2327]

Before we leave this subject, let us mention two things:

**First:** One of the signs of the soundness of a knowledge, *dhikr*, or study session is that a person leaves it in a better state and with softer faith. But this is only felt by one with a sound heart; as for the diseased heart, no regard is given to its feelings so long as it is diseased.

**Second:** We mentioned gathering in this chapter as a stimulant for the *sayr*, and it is something palpable. Let each of us try himself when he is in a period of lassitude and heedlessness—that is, when his Qur’anic litanies and others are not regular, or his soul is averse to them—let such a person try attending a *dhikr*, knowledge, or study session with a righteous man, and then observe afterward his soul’s turning toward Allah. It is likely that his turning will be greater; indeed, for many people gathering is almost a new point of departure. Perhaps this is one of the secrets of the obligatoriness of the Friday prayer and its sermon. Therefore it is of great importance for the Muslim, in normal circumstances, to have a connection with circles of knowledge and *dhikr*, and with study sessions with righteous people; and the shaykhs of Muslims must arrange that.

Let us turn to the second stimulant in the *sayr* to Allah, Mighty and Majestic.

## 2. Inshād

The *hadāʾ* (حداء) was known in the life of the Messenger of Allah; some Companions sang during work, and some during travel. The Messenger of Allah sometimes joined in the *hadāʾ*. The Companions composed poetry, and some of this poetry was recited or sung, by slave-girls or by men during travel or work.

It was customary among the Arabs to sing poetry. Among their sayings: “Sing poetry, whether you are its author or another’s.” But the predominant listening of the Companions was listening to the Noble Qur’an. And listening to poetry—whether recited or sung—existed, but only on an occasion, or at a time of rest, or at a time of joy, or at a wedding.

In the hadith narrated by Ibn Kathīr from ʿAbdullah ibn Masʿūd, may Allah be pleased with him, who said: The Messenger of Allah said: “Do not let me find one of you placing one leg over the other, singing and leaving Sūrat al-Baqarah unread; for Satan flees from the house in which Sūrat al-Baqarah is recited. And the houses that are most empty are those empty of the Book of Allah.” [Ibn Mardawayh; Sunan al-Nasāʾī, *al-ʿAmal al-Yawm wa-al-Laylah*]

This confirms that the original thing to listen to in the life of the Companions was the Qur’an. Poetry had its place, but it was like salt in food in their lives. As for *inshād*, it too had a place with them, but it was little in their lives which were full of grave matters.

This is the first point to be taken against some *ṣūfiyyah*: that *inshād* and delight in beautiful voices took a large share of their lives, more than can be compared to what was in the lives of the Companions, may Allah be pleased with them.

In the life of the Companions we find poetry expressing their daily life, whether in their struggle with disbelief, or in expressing their longings; it covers the whole Islamic life and moves a group of Islamic feelings. Sometimes it stirs feelings of *jihād* (جهاد), sometimes it expresses longing for homeland, sometimes pride in Islam, sometimes warm elegy, sometimes turning toward Allah. Many *ṣūfiyyah* restricted the circles of *inshād* to a kind of meanings that stir some wholesome emotions, but do not stir all the emotions that should be stirred in the Muslim. The Islamic movement compensated for this deficiency, but it neglected stirring emotions of love for the Islamic homeland, spiritual rapture, and other things—although the teacher al-Bannā had a role in this, and he would sometimes do it, as those who knew him told us, may Allah have mercy on him.

Symbolism, allusion, and metonymy have a place in the poetry of the Arabs and others. They may express a spiritual meaning in a sensory style, and they may cite a line originally addressed to one entity, addressing another with it. The Arabs have many methods of address and imagination: they address the dead as if imagining him alive, and address the inanimate as if it understands; all of that exists in their poetry.

From the poetry of the Prophetic era: the words of Zayd ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, longing for the Messenger of Allah while on his deathbed:

> Would that the women who call me to them had not called me,  
> and would that those who turned me from my love had turned me not.

And Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr said to his brother Bujayr:

> The Trusted One gave you to drink a cup of full wine,  
> so the Trusted One made it forbidden and lawful for you.

Here he compared the punishment that Bujayr received to wine being drunk, and he compared the Messenger of Allah to a cup-bearer; all of this is part of the Arabs’ method of address.

From these meanings the *ṣūfiyyah* proceeded to express spiritual realities, likening them to sensory things. They used the word “wine” to express meanings, and the word “intoxication” to express meanings; then they expanded and expanded until much disapproval came upon them from ignorant and learned alike, and they ended up, as a result of expansions, in *shirk* (شرك) and *kufr* (كفر). Long discussions occurred in many matters as a result.

There is no doubt that the breadth of the Arabic language and its methods of expression help many to slip away from any hold taken by the literalists; and there is no doubt that literalism in literary and emotional matters is not the best way of understanding. There is a limit accepted by knowledge, not disapproved by the literalist, and not leading the common person to understand mistaken concepts. This is the limit that must be sought, adopted, disseminated, and established.

The *ṣūfiyyah* noticed, when they composed the *nashīd* (نشيد), that when the truth is presented to the soul in a way that gives it pleasure, its acceptance of the truth is better. Therefore they considered *inshād*, for the beginner, analogous to medicine: through his soul’s inclination to beautiful sound, he may absorb some meanings from it. They also noticed that the *nashīd* is like a scale by which a person weighs how much meaning he has—love for Allah and His Messenger, and other sublime meanings. They tried to fashion the whole *sayr* to Allah as poetry, and through listening to this poetry a person knows his station, and his resolve moves toward what is higher.

There is no doubt that singing and poetry have effects in shaping human emotions. The *ṣūfiyyah* succeeded in strengthening many emotions through *nashīd*, but they missed some of them. Their role was great in enabling them to create a kind of alternative to corrupt matters. Thus it became established among people in later ages that the people of corruption gather in their joys, conviviality, and pleasures to singing and music, while *inshād* and listening among the people of good is the alternative to all that.

The Messenger of Allah encouraged *inshād* at weddings, out of consideration for the temperament of the Anṣār—something that can be taken as a principle in this subject.

Throughout Islamic history many things attached themselves to *inshād*; many things became mixed with it; every group and every land developed a style of *nashīd* or customs connected with *inshād*; indeed, every *ṭarīqah* (طريقة) had a kind of *nashīd* that was its badge. And throughout all that there was a long struggle between jurists and the people of these circles over meanings expressed or customs introduced.

The people of our age inherited all of this. And as with much good that we have inherited, chaff is mixed with it. The subject of *inshād* became connected with the issue of celebrating the birth of the Messenger of Allah. People took two positions on this matter: a strict position disapproving of people gathering for such a thing, and a favorable position; a long battle took place and still takes place because of it.

If you analyze the issue of the *mawlid* (مولد), you find it amounts either to narrating passages from the Sīrah, or reciting a line of poetry concerning the Messenger of Allah—and this, if one grants what is disapproved of it for general reasons, is not blameworthy. Even Ibn Taymiyyah, may Allah have mercy on him, perceived the good meanings resulting from gathering for the *mawlid* that make people deserving of reward. And even Ibn al-Ḥājj in *al-Madkhal*—one of the strictest people against innovations—considered that the Muslims have a third ʿeid for which there is no [explicit] text, namely the Mawlid, taking from the words of the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him:

> That is a day on which I was born. [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Fasting, Hadith 1162]

Practical reality testifies that the celebration of the Muslims for the birth of the Messenger of Allah contains, in remembrance, repentance, and instruction, effects that cannot be counted. Al-Bannā considered the revival of Islamic occasions and reminding people of them among the pillars of the Islamic movement. Therefore it is almost self-evident in the contemporary *fiqh* of Islamic *daʿwah* to give the issue of the Prophetic birth and its celebration a studied, generally acceptable, jurisprudentially sound form of great importance.

After all this we can say:

- *Inshād*, in the contemporary *fiqh* of Islamic *daʿwah*, has its place, but it remains like medicine: in narrow limits and for the sick, not as food.
- It is necessary to choose carefully what is sung in our circles so that it covers the breadth of Islamic emotions and does not depart from what is pleasing to the jurist. This requires the jurist to participate in selecting poetry for the singer, and we should not permit the singer to say whatever he wishes in our circles.
- If *inshād* observes these meanings and is not taken beyond its proper place, it becomes an incentive to the *sayr* to Allah with all its requirements: from desire for the Hereafter, to encouragement of *jihād*, to steadfastness on the path, to stimulation to work, to affirmation of the struggle against disbelief—these are palpable matters denied only by one narrow in outlook.
- We should select for Islamic occasions kinds of poetry in which both meaning and performance are observed, as part of a comprehensive program that achieves near or distant goals.

Within this whole framework we understand the issue of *inshād*, and in light of that we consider it among the stimulants of the *sayr* to Allah. As for what lies outside that, we have observations on it. For example, frequent listening and taking pleasure in beautiful voices, even if it is *nashīd*, creates in some a psychological relaxation. This relaxation may lead to neglect of duties, or readiness to fall into desires. So listening is sometimes food for the heart, and sometimes food for the *nafs*.

Therefore the author of *al-Mabāḥith al-Aṣliyyah* said: “As for singing, it is permitted for the ascetics and recommended for young shaykhs; but for the great shaykhs it is like the unlawful.” Some shaykhs see no harm in listening, but they fear its effect on the souls of their listeners, in that it produces relaxation from enjoining good and forbidding evil. Some shaykhs feared a slip of the human being in listening, such as carrying a meaning appropriate for shaykhs but not appropriate for the Messenger of Allah—[applying it] to the Messenger of Allah—or carrying a meaning not appropriate to Allah, [applying it] to Allah. All of this must be observed.

We shall return more than once to the subject of *inshād*; let us stop here and mention the third stimulant of the *sayr*: reading in the books of the *sayr* to Allah and the stories of the wayfarers.

## 3. Reading in the Books of the Sayr to Allah and the Stories of the Righteous

There are some *ṣūfī* imams whom the Ummah unanimously accepted: such as Junayd, may Allah have mercy on him, and such as Shaykh ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlānī, may Allah have mercy on him, concerning whom Ibn Taymiyyah, may Allah have mercy on him, said that his miracles are transmitted to us by *tawātur* (تواتر). When such imams are mentioned before a person, his heart is stirred; and his resolve is moved by that. There are other imams whom the majority accepted, and some of them discussed certain matters, such as Ḥujjat al-Islām al-Ghazālī, may Allah have mercy on him, of whom al-ʿAqqād said that the whole world, East and West, has not known his like as a thinker; and such as Ibn al-Qayyim, may Allah have mercy on him. Each of these two wrote at the summit in some matters of the *sayr* to Allah. And the insightful scholar in the religion of Allah does not miss the places of correct criticism; no one is infallible except the Messenger of Allah.

Reading in the books of these imams and jurists who perfected themselves in the matter of the *sayr* to Allah stirs the resolve toward Allah. This is something empirically obvious; every person can perceive it through experience. Let one of us take the first part of *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*, for example, and read in it the book “The Marvels of the Qur’an,” and then try to read the Qur’an afterward. He will undoubtedly find that the presence of his heart with the Qur’an has changed from what it was before. And the same is true of every study by al-Ghazālī; when you read it you find yourself transported to another state.

Reading in the books of the *sayr* to Allah inclines one to the *sayr* to Allah and helps one to converse with it. The wayfarer should help, stimulate, incite, and arouse himself to the *sayr* by reading books of the *sayr* to Allah—such as *al-Risālah al-Qushayriyyah* and *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*. Likewise, reading the stories of the *sālikīn* to Allah has a great effect in raising resolve. In the books *Ṣifat al-Ṣafwah* or *Ḥilyat al-Awliyāʾ* there is provision for the *sālik*.

We have observations here:

1. Most books of *tasawwuf* do not put one at ease with many of their jurisprudential expressions; therefore one must be precise in choosing what one reads, and when one reads, one must scrutinize.
2. Some books that presented stories of the righteous contain deviations that do not agree with reason or Shariʿah—things we consider the pen too noble to mention, and we consider the imams to whom these books are attributed too noble to have spoken such things. We must be alert to that.
3. Many people overindulged in studying books of the *sayr* to Allah and reading books of the righteous until they forgot the Book, the *Sunnah*, the Sīrah of the Messenger of Allah, and the life of the Companions. Therefore this matter must be given its due; no study may be at the expense of neglecting the Book, the *Sunnah*, the Sīrah, and the life of the Companions.

Let us stop with this much.

## Practical Suggestions

In the three preceding sections we presented three stimulants in the subject of the *sayr* to Allah, and we presented some criticisms of what exists in some of them, so that the Muslim may be insightful in taking. It may be appropriate to conclude this chapter with practical suggestions in this regard to be before the callers to Allah and the shaykhs of Muslims:

1. I hope that multiple circles be established in every mosque: circles of *dhikr*, circles of knowledge, and that *inshād* have its role sometimes at the end of some circles.
2. There are circles whose establishment requires many conditions, and there are circles whose establishment does not require such conditions. So we must exert our utmost effort to establish circles in light of what is available to us.
3. The following sessions can be established in every mosque:

   - A *dhikr* session and a session of sending blessings upon the Messenger of Allah. The two sessions may be combined, as follows: It begins after ʿAṣr prayer on Friday, or on another day. The attendees begin individually, each sending blessings upon the Messenger of Allah with the formula they are comfortable with. The formula that fulfills the minimum of the command to send blessings upon him is our saying: “O Allah, send blessings upon Muḥammad and his family and his Companions.” A specific time may be fixed, such as a third of an hour, or a specific number, so as not to burden the attendees. Then after that *dhikr* begins while we are seated, such as our saying: “Subḥān Allāh, al-ḥamdulillāh, lā ilāha illā Allāh, Allāhu akbar,” about one hundred times. Then there may be some selected poetry with *inshād*. Then the session ends with some Qur’an recitation. The *inshād* portion may be omitted if its conditions are not met. The concern in the session is that it not be long, and that it contain nothing that could form a juristic objection.

   - A Qur’anic session: The people sit in the mosque after a prayer; then portions of the Qur’an are distributed among them, so that each reads a portion covering a verse or two, or more or less according to the number. After each has read his portion within a specified time, some of them read aloud with measured recitation. Then there is a light lesson, such as reading some prophetic *aḥādīth* from a book like *Riyāḍ al-Ṣāliḥīn*, or reading a passage from the Sīrah, then supplication and departure.

   - A sermon session: The session begins with *dhikr*—“Subḥān Allāh, al-ḥamdulillāh, lā ilāha illā Allāh, Allāhu akbar”—one hundred times, then there is an exhortation lesson, then some *inshād*, then some Qur’an recitation, supplication, and departure. Some portions may be put before others.

4. A committee in every mosque should follow up the matter of public and private circles, so that in every mosque there is movement toward fulfilling individual obligations and producing specialists who cover the collective religious obligations in the area. If a mosque lacks some means—such as a shaykh to direct some circles, or someone to teach some sciences—the people of the mosque must seek someone to help them, and others must do likewise. You see Muslims eager to form committees for repairing the mosque building or building mosques, without doing the same for the work of the mosque for the sake of which the mosque was founded. This is a situation that must be completed. Competition should arise between mosques and their people in arranging the work of mosques outwardly and inwardly.
5. A committee in the mosque—voluntary, elected, or appointed—should undertake reviving Islamic occasions, such as reviving the Mawlid and arranging it so that it yields great benefit in understanding the Sīrah of the Messenger of Allah, reminding Muslims of their Islam, and binding them to the mosque. And reviving the occasions of the Hijrah, and the occasion of the liberation of Jerusalem from the Crusaders on 27 Rajab—which is the day the Muslims celebrate the anniversary of the Isrāʾ and Miʿrāj—and reminding Muslims in the seasons, the season of Ramaḍān and the days of Ḥajj, and reminding them of the right of the first ten days of Dhū al-Ḥijjah.
6. Many of these things can be arranged and held in homes in addition to the mosque, indeed the preparation for matters of reviving the mosque can be done in homes.

If we could create such atmospheres in mosques and homes, we would have prepared opportunities for every wayfarer to journey to Allah in a kind of *sayr*, by providing all the conditions that attract to the *sayr*, or urge it, or stimulate it. This requires every Muslim, whatever his condition and whatever his occupations, to exert effort in this path by participating, inviting, attending, and encouraging it with himself, his wealth, and his tongue.
