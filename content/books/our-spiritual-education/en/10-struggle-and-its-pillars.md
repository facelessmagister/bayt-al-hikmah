# Chapter 10: Spiritual Struggle and Its Pillars

Allah the Exalted says:

> And those who strive for Us—We will surely guide them to Our ways. [Qur'an, Surah al-'Ankabut 29:69]

From this verse we understand that the beginning of the paths leading to Allah and His pleasure is the effect of *mujāhadah* (مجاهدة). Thus *mujāhadah* is the effort of man, and *hidāyah* (guidance) is a gift from Allah to man; and both *mujāhadah* and *hidāyah* do not come about except by Allah's enabling grace, help, and support. For this reason our Lord taught us to say in our prayer: *"It is You we worship and You we ask for help."*

*Mujāhadah* is the means of the heart's guidance to Allah and His pleasure, and *hidāyah* is the prerequisite of *taqwā* (تقوى, God-consciousness). Allah the Exalted says:

> And as for those who were guided, He increased them in guidance and gave them their righteousness. [Qur'an, Surah Muḥammad 47:17]

So the sequence is as follows: *mujāhadah* leads to *hidāyah*, and *hidāyah* leads to *taqwā*, and all of that happens only by Allah's enabling grace, help, and gift. From here we realize that the correct starting point in the journey to Allah is *mujāhadah*. For this reason the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "The *mujāhid* is one who strives against his own soul in Allah's cause." [Sunan al-Tirmidhī]

And if this is the *mujāhid*, then guidance to the paths—including fighting in Allah's cause—does not come without *mujāhadah*. Consequently, fighting itself is not acceptable fighting except after *hidāyah*, and there is no *hidāyah* except after *mujāhadah*, unless your Lord wills to give His servant without a [discernible] cause.

In these circles there are many errors. There are people whose conception of *mujāhadah* is mistaken. There are people who stop at *mujāhadah* and do not reach the paths. Allah the Exalted says:

> There has come to you from Allah a light and a clear Book, by which Allah guides those who pursue His pleasure to the ways of peace and brings them out from darkness into the light, by His permission, and guides them to a straight path. [Qur'an, Surah al-Māʾidah 5:15–16]

They busy themselves with what they imagine to be *mujāhadah*, yet do not reach the paths by understanding them and walking in their ways. And there are people who ascend from *mujāhadah* to traveling the paths, yet do not reach the reality of *taqwā*. There is a deficiency in understanding, in aptitude, or in conduct.

All of that originates from ignorance. Therefore, there must be an understanding of the issue of *mujāhadah*, of the issue of *taqwā*, and of the issue of the paths. The subject is interwoven at its beginnings and endings, with many connections. Knowledge of *taqwā* is part of *mujāhadah*; and *taqwā* itself is partly an effect of *mujāhadah*, and partly part of *mujāhadah*. In the book *Soldiers of Allah: Culture and Morality* there is a broad explanation of these matters, so let the reader consult it.

Here, however, we intend to sketch a picture of the issue of *mujāhadah* of the soul in its general foundations, by which the soul is freed from its diseases and realizes the meanings of its health. We assume that the wayfarer on this path is one who has already grasped the general obligations from beginning to end, so let him bear that in mind.

## The Starting Point of *Mujāhadah*

*Mujāhadah* begins from the point of faith in Allah and His oneness, and that Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah. Some modern Muslim living in an Islamic environment may not feel that this falls under the chapter of *mujāhadah*—and this is a grave error. For the greatest thing requiring *mujāhadah* is for a person to leap from disbelief to faith, or to declare his faith in an environment that rejects faith, an environment that expels him from his family. Allah the Exalted says:

> And whoever believes in Allah—He guides his heart. [Qur'an, Surah al-Taghābun 64:11]

Then comes the second stage of *mujāhadah*: performing the time-bound obligations—prayer when its time arrives, fasting Ramaḍān when it comes, paying *zakāh* when the lunar year passes, performing *ḥajj* when its season arrives and one is able, marrying when sexual drives become strong and marriage is feasible for the person, regulating a transaction such as sale or lease according to the Sharīʿah if one is engaged in it, maintaining family ties and honoring parents if there are relatives and parents, and other time-bound obligations. Every person has obligations of his time that may coincide with those of others or differ according to his condition, circumstances, and so forth. There is a sick person unable to fast, so fasting is not an obligation for him in that state. There is a person who owns no wealth, so *zakāh* is not due from him. There is a person whose parents have died, so he is not obligated with respect to honoring them in this matter—rather, there are other duties incumbent upon him.

After observing the obligation of the time, one must observe the adab (proper conduct) of the time. What is the adab of the morning, the time before dawn, the time of sunset? And what is the adab of a person when he is traveling, or sick, or at a banquet, or in prison, or with a group, or at school, or in a shop, or at play, or at a wedding, or in mourning? These are matters connected to the obligations of the time.

## The Second Aspect: Guarding the Soul

As an application of the subject of time-bound obligations and their adab, there is the restraint of the soul from forbidden and disliked things that the soul desires or that the wayfarer encounters during his journey. This is a second aspect of *mujāhadah*.

## The Third Aspect: Supererogatory Works

A third part of *mujāhadah* concerns what a person imposes upon himself of supererogatory acts of worship: prayer, *zakāh*, fasting, *iʿtikāf*, *ḥajj*, supplications, remembrances, and Qur'an recitation. Included in this are the spiritual retreats and daily devotional practices mentioned earlier. This is the third aspect.

## The Four Pillars of *Mujāhadah*

Then comes the fourth issue, which we call the pillars of *mujāhadah*. Those who spoke about the pillars of *mujāhadah* mentioned four pillars: *ʿuzlah* (solitude), *ṣamt* (silence), *sahar* (wakefulness), and *jūʿ* (hunger). We shall discuss them briefly so that the brother may return, if he desires detail, to the extensive books such as *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn* and others.

Then comes the fifth issue: reflecting upon the soul and the heart, discovering diseases, and treating them. This is the final issue in *mujāhadah* and one of its principal fruits. The last two issues will be treated in detail in what follows; they are the matters around which many people revolve when they speak of *mujāhadah*. In this chapter we shall suffice with mentioning the pillars of *mujāhadah*, and in the following chapter we shall address the treatment of diseases.

So let us begin speaking about the four pillars of *mujāhadah*, and let us begin with *ʿuzlah*.

## 1. *ʿUzlah* (Solitude)

Solitude from people is not the original state in the life of a Muslim. Rather, wholesome companionship, good association, and affection for good and its people are the original state in the life of a Muslim. The following *aḥādīth* confirm what we have said:

> The believer who mixes with people and bears their harm is better than the one who does not mix with people and does not bear their harm. [Sunan Ibn Mājah with a good chain]

> Let one who loves and is loved be, for there is no good in one who neither loves nor is loved. [Sunan Ibn Mājah with a good chain]

> The hand of Allah is with the group; whoever shies away, shies away toward the Fire. [Sunan al-Tirmidhī]

> The wolf eats only the stray sheep. [Sunan Abū Dāwūd, narration of Abū Dāwūd; sound]

An essential aspect of this original state in Muslim life is that one avoid disbelief, transgression, immorality, and their people, and shun gatherings in which the signs of Allah are mocked and the like. Solitude from such things is what is fitting. Allah the Exalted says, on the tongue of Ibrāhīm, peace be upon him:

> And I will leave you and what you invoke besides Allah and will invoke my Lord. It is expected that I will not be, in invoking my Lord, unhappy. [Qur'an, Surah Maryam 19:48]

And He said:

> There has already been for you an excellent pattern in Ibrāhīm and those with him, when they said to their people, "Indeed, we are disassociated from you and from whatever you worship other than Allah. We have denied you, and there has appeared between us and you animosity and hatred forever until you believe in Allah alone." [Qur'an, Surah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:4]

And Allah the Exalted says:

> And when you see those who engage in [offensive] discourse concerning Our verses, turn away from them until they enter into another conversation. And if Satan should cause you to forget, then do not remain, after remembrance, with the wrongdoing people. [Qur'an, Surah al-Anʿām 6:68]

And the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "The companion of the righteous is like the owner of musk: even if you do not receive anything from him, you receive the fragrance. And the companion of the wicked is like the owner of a bellows: even if you do not receive any of its blackness, you receive its smoke." [Sahīh al-Bukhārī and Sahīh Muslim]

And he, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "The example of the one who sits with the righteous is like the one who sits with the perfume-seller..." [Sahīh al-Bukhārī]

And he said: "The example of the hypocrite is like a sheep wandering between two flocks, now joining this one and now joining that one." [Sahīh Muslim]

And the jurists have disliked associating with the corrupt and socializing with them.

From all of this we understand what is the original state for the Muslim in the matter of companionship and solitude. Perhaps the clearest thing in this regard is his statement, peace and blessings be upon him, to Ḥuḍhayfah when he asked him: "What do you command me if that [trial] overtakes me?" He said: "Adhere to the congregation of the Muslims and their imām." He said: "What if there is no congregation of Muslims and no imām?" He said: "Then abandon all those sects (i.e., sects of misguidance), even if you must bite onto the root of a tree until death overtakes you while you are in that state." [Sahīh Muslim]

Thus: no solitude from the people of truth, and solitude is entirely from misguidance and its people. This is the general original state in the life of a Muslim regarding companionship and solitude.

When this original state is clear, we understand when absolute solitude becomes obligatory in the life of a Muslim, and when it becomes obligatory, he must strive against his soul to bear it, for it is the nature of the soul to be accustomed to human companionship. But if we reflect on the noble *ḥadīth*: "There will not cease to be a group of my ummah manifest upon the truth..." [Sahīh al-Bukhārī and Sahīh Muslim], we realize that the states in which absolute solitude is obligatory upon a person are occasional, emergent, or temporary. Therefore, in the context of the journey to Allah we discuss the issue of solitude as one of the pillars of *mujāhadah*—as a remedy for the heart and soul of man, a necessity for it at times in the life of a Muslim. This is what we mean, and this is the utmost we consider for the Muslim in this chapter, except when there is a special circumstance or an emergent condition. For a fatwā takes into account time, place, and person.

### Solitude as Remedy

So the subject we examine here, then, is solitude as a remedy for the heart, and its place in *mujāhadah*. Let us mention some expressions of the Sufis in this regard.

Ibn ʿAṭāʾ said: "Bury your existence in the earth of annihilation, for what is not buried does not yield its fruit. Nothing has benefited the heart like solitude through which it enters the field of reflection. How can the heart in which the forms of created things are impressed shine? How can it journey to Allah while shackled by its desires? How can it hope to enter the presence of Allah when it has not purified itself from the impurity of heedlessness? How can it hope to understand the subtleties of the secrets when it has not repented from its lapses?"

In these words Ibn ʿAṭāʾ summarized the various meanings for which man needs solitude as a remedy. When a person becomes well-known, his connections multiply; when his connections multiply, much of his time is lost because of them; when much of his time is lost, it becomes difficult for him to nurture his soul in knowledge, action, and spiritual state. For this condition solitude is sought. When a person is alone with himself and turns his thought to the kingdom of Allah, the dominion of the All-Merciful, solitude becomes a garden of intimacy with Allah. And as long as a person is in society, the purity of his heart is weak and the impression of things upon it is strong; solitude accompanied by reflection and remembrance helps him to polish the mirror of his heart. As long as a person is in society, many stimuli of desires may pull his heart, and solitude cuts him off from such things; that assists his heart in liberating itself from the bondage of desires—so this is another aspect that solitude helps. And as long as a person is in society, heedlessness overcomes him; if solitude with remembrance and reflection is granted to him, this helps awaken his heart. As long as the heart is much in society, it is much given to lapses, and this stands between it and understanding the subtleties of secrets. Solitude helps it to free itself from the lapses of the heart and qualifies it to understand the subtleties of secrets.

These are some of the meanings for which comprehensive solitude, or partial solitude as part of *mujāhadah* of the soul—indeed as a pillar of it—has been prescribed. It should be observed that all of this should be stage-bound in a person's life, not at the expense of time-bound obligations and their adab, nor of neglecting rights. Perhaps in the Messenger of Allah's seclusion in the Cave of Ḥirāʾ before revelation there is a model for total solitude. And in the *sunnah* of *iʿtikāf* there is a model for partial solitude.

In any case, solitude, when it involves no neglect of rights or duties, is of the permissible things, even if no benefit results from it. But if benefits result from it—such as reforming the heart, acquiring knowledge, or increasing faith—then it ascends from the permissible to something finer.

If it becomes a means of fulfilling an obligation or of freeing oneself from something forbidden, it assumes the character of an obligation. And all thinkers in general find in solitude an opportunity for reflection and refinement of thought. Therefore, disapproval of one who practices temporary solitude—total or partial—to cure a disease or to achieve a general or religious benefit, so long as it is not at the expense of a right or the adab of time, is itself an error. The insight of one who disapproves is weak, and his intellectual horizons narrow.

We shall content ourselves with this much in pointing to the first pillar of *mujāhadah* in the terminology of the wayfarers to Allah. Let us move to the second pillar of *mujāhadah* in the terminology of the wayfarers to Allah, which is *ṣamt* (silence).

## 2. *Ṣamt* (Silence)

The refinement of the tongue in Islam is one of the most important matters absolutely. For this reason we find that the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "Whoever guarantees for me what is between his jaws—meaning his tongue—and what is between his legs, I will guarantee for him Paradise." [Sunan al-Tirmidhī, sound]

And he, peace and blessings be upon him, said to Muʿādh: "Shall I not inform you of the key to all good?" He said: "Yes, O Messenger of Allah." He said: "Restrain this," and he pointed to his tongue. Then he said: "And we are taken to account for what we speak." Muʿādh said: "May my father and mother be ransomed for you, O Messenger of Allah! Will people really be thrown on their faces into the Fire because of what their tongues harvest?" He said: "Yes." [Sunan al-Tirmidhī, sound]

So restraining the tongue according to the Sharīʿah of Allah is one of the most important and most difficult matters for man. The original rule concerning the tongue is that man should not use it except for good, and that he restrain it from every evil, indeed from idle speech. The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "Whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent." [Sahīh al-Bukhārī and Sahīh Muslim]

And Allah the Exalted says:

> No good is there in much of their private conversation, except one who enjoins charity or that which is right or conciliation between people. And whoever does that seeking the pleasure of Allah—then We will give him a great reward. [Qur'an, Surah al-Nisāʾ 4:114]

And Allah the Exalted says:

> And speak to people good words. [Qur'an, Surah al-Baqarah 2:83]

And:

> And cooperate in righteousness and piety. [Qur'an, Surah al-Māʾidah 5:2]

The ailments of the tongue that a Muslim must guard his tongue against are many; al-Ghazālī mentioned them in his *Iḥyāʾ* and enumerated them, so let the reader consult it there.

Thus the original rule in the matter of the tongue is that man protect it from the two spheres of sin and idle talk, and use it in the sphere of good. Distinguishing between what is good and evil, what is idle and true, requires broad knowledge and much self-restraint.

The tongue is the first instrument of expression for the soul. The soul is inclined toward many things, and the tongue is the nearest path to expressing those things. How many are the things to which the soul is inclined that are not proper to appear on the tongue! The soul is inclined to boasting, inclined to insulting and arguing when angry, inclined to conversing even in idle talk, inclined to belittling others, inclined to making others aware of its own virtue. All of that and its like are abundant matters in which the Muslim should not give his soul free rein. He must train his soul to restrain its tongue in that and similar things. The prelude to all of that is mastery over the tongue, and the prelude to mastery over the tongue is accustoming oneself to silence. Then one gradually progresses with one's soul until it becomes accustomed to disciplined speech.

Whoever does not accustom himself to silence will find it difficult to weigh his words before speaking. This is one of the meanings that caused people to regard accustoming oneself to silence as part of *mujāhadah* and as a necessity in the journey to Allah, the Mighty and Majestic.

A person may be able to speak words that are good, but it may not be wise to say them. For example, to warn people of Allah's displeasure and remind them of His Fire is good, but if you do that at a meal it is not wise. For this reason the jurists disliked a person mentioning such matters in such a state, because that conflicts with the adab of the gathering. This example clarifies how a word may be good yet not wise. This is a vast subject that no one can master except by Allah's enabling grace. For this reason Allah the Exalted says:

> And whoever is given wisdom has certainly been given much good. [Qur'an, Surah al-Baqarah 2:269]

Accustoming oneself to silence is a prelude to becoming accustomed to weighing one's words before uttering them. This is a second wisdom in making silence a pillar of *mujāhadah*.

There is no doubt that the tongue is one of the principal and greatest avenues of error. If a person succeeds in restraining it, he will have covered a great distance in refining his soul and setting it upright. Silence is a prelude to restraint; if one succeeds in silence, it is likely that he will succeed in disciplined speech by Allah's enabling grace.

Finally, if we recall the noble *ḥadīth*: "If it were not for the softness of your hearts and your love of excessive talk, I would have conversed with what is with me," we would find that excessive talk is one of the factors that veil the heart from the unseen. For this reason silence is a path to the tenderness of the heart. All these meanings make silence one of the pillars of *mujāhadah*.

But what silence? The silence that is a remedy and a prelude to restraining the tongue is stage-bound silence, silence when speech is neither obligatory nor prescribed. But if speech is obligatory or prescribed—such as commanding good, forbidding evil, or teaching a necessary matter—then silence at that point is forbidden. Within these conditions silence becomes good as part of a stage in a person's life. Silence is a means, not an end; a stage in life until the soul is set right, not for all of life.

In light of all that, we understand the issue of silence as a pillar of *mujāhadah* for the soul. Let us move to the third pillar of *mujāhadah*, which is *jūʿ* (hunger).

## 3. *Jūʿ* (Hunger)

The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said in the *ḥadīth* narrated by al-Ṭabarānī with a good chain: "Adhere to sadness, for it is the key to the heart." They said: "O Messenger of Allah, and what is sadness?" He said: "Humble your souls with hunger and thirst." From this *ḥadīth* we see how hunger can be a remedy for the soul in some of its states and diseases.

And he, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "O young men, whoever among you is able to marry, let him marry, for it lowers the gaze and guards the chastity; and whoever is not able, let him fast, for it is a shield for him." [Sahīh al-Bukhārī and Sahīh Muslim] From this *ḥadīth* also you know how hunger can be a remedy for the soul in some of its states.

If it becomes clear from these two *aḥādīth* that hunger can be a treatment for some states of the soul, then we have laid the foundation by which we understand the idea of accustoming oneself to hunger as a pillar of *mujāhadah* in a stage of life and in the stages of the journey to Allah. So let us examine more broadly what deepens our understanding of this issue.

The general rule in Islam regarding food is that eating and drinking in the amount that satisfies a person's needs so that he is able to perform obligations and duties is obligatory. Eating to this extent is an obligation. Expanding in food to the point of satiety is permissible. Excess in it is forbidden. Allah the Exalted says:

> And eat and drink, but be not excessive. Indeed, He does not like those who commit excess. [Qur'an, Surah al-Aʿrāf 7:31]

Excess is a relative matter that differs according to people and their conditions, and according to ages and economic circumstances. And although eating to satiety is permissible, this is conditional upon not giving the soul every craving, for that conflicts with Islamic taste and the general spirituality of Muslims. Moreover, the texts indicate obesity as a blight in Muslim society. In the *ḥadīth* censuring a wicked generation that comes after a righteous predecessor: "They will desire martyrdom but not attain it, and they will betray but not be faithful, and there will appear among them obesity..." Thus expanding in food and inclining bodily matters until a person reaches obesity is a blameworthy matter in Islamic society, and the texts are clear on that.

From all of this we understand that although eating to satiety is permissible, perpetual satiety in the life of a Muslim is not the original state. For this reason we find the authentic *ḥadīth*: "The son of Adam has no vessel worse than his stomach. It is sufficient for him to eat what keeps his back upright. But if he must have more, then one-third for food, one-third for drink, and one-third for air." [Sunan al-Tirmidhī, sound]

This is the original state generally in the life of a Muslim. If a Muslim neglects this original state, his soul becomes corrupted or difficult for him to restrain; then he must treat all of that with hunger, by fasting or otherwise. Likewise, if he is afflicted with pathological obesity as a result of neglecting his soul, he must treat himself with hunger that does no harm, or with a regimen by which he rids himself of this condition.

And since hunger is a treatment and satiety is permissible, one must observe harm in both states: anything that leads to certain bodily harm is forbidden, and anything that leads to probable harm is disliked. In light of all that, we understand the issue of hunger as a pillar of *mujāhadah*. And do not forget that fasting, as part of this *mujāhadah*, is the finest of it.

## 4. *Sahar* (Wakefulness)

The remaining fourth pillar of *mujāhadah* is *sahar* (wakefulness).

If a Muslim does not control his sleep, serious negligence may result in many matters. The Fajr prayer in congregation may be endangered; seeking forgiveness in the last part of the night may be endangered; *qiyām al-layl* (night prayer) and *tahajjud* may be lost; the ʿIshāʾ prayer in congregation, and the devotional practices after Fajr, and many things may be disrupted because of a person's failure to organize his sleep and train his soul to control the matter of sleep. This is especially so in our age, in which Western ways of life have prevailed over our lands. The Westerner finishes his work and sleeps; then his chance for amusement and pleasure comes, so he stays up late into the night, then sleeps until late hours before going to work. This is the prevalent condition there, and it has become the prevalent condition for many of us as well because of cinema, radio news broadcasts, and the like. With this condition many obligations, supererogatory acts, and Islamic customs are lost.

The matter of sleep in every age needs treatment and control; in our age the need for it is greater.

Night in Islam has a special status. Allah the Exalted says:

> Indeed, the hours of the night are more effective for concurrence [of heart and tongue] and more suitable for words. [Qur'an, Surah al-Muzzammil 73:6]

For a person to rise in worship at night is hard for him, and for that he has reward. Night prayer has a purity not possessed by other prayers, an effect upon the soul not possessed by other acts, and an understanding of meanings not possessed by other times. This verse came in the context of His saying, the Exalted:

> O you who wraps himself [in clothing], arise [to pray] the night, except a little—half of it or subtract from it a little, or add to it, and recite the Qur'an with measured recitation. Indeed, We will cast upon you a heavy word. Indeed, the hours of the night are more effective for concurrence and more suitable for words. Indeed, for you by day is prolonged occupation. And remember the name of your Lord and devote yourself to Him with [complete] devotion. [Qur'an, Surah al-Muzzammil 73:1–8]

Night in Islam has a status, and these verses suffice to realize that. Among the manifestations of this status is what we find in the following *aḥādīth*:

Al-Tirmidhī narrated with a good chain: "It was said: O Messenger of Allah, which supplication is most likely to be answered? He said: "In the last third of the night, and at the backs of the prescribed prayers." [Sunan al-Tirmidhī]

And the Six [authentic collections] except al-Nasāʾī narrated from the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him: "Our Lord descends every night to the lowest heaven when the last third of the night remains and says: 'Who will call upon Me, that I may answer him? Who will ask of Me, that I may give him? Who will seek forgiveness of Me, that I may forgive him?'" [Sahīh al-Bukhārī and Sahīh Muslim]

*Qiyām al-layl* has a status in Islam, as do supplication and seeking forgiveness in the last third of the night, as do the ʿIshāʾ and Fajr prayers in congregation, and the devotional practices after Fajr.

> Whoever prays ʿIshāʾ in congregation, it is as if he prayed half the night; and whoever prays Fajr in congregation, it is as if he prayed the whole night. [Sahīh Muslim]

> These two prayers, Fajr and ʿIshāʾ, are the heaviest upon the hypocrites. If they knew what is in them, they would come to them even if they had to crawl. [Sahīh al-Bukhārī and Sahīh Muslim]

> Whoever prays Fajr in congregation, then sits remembering Allah until the sun rises, then prays two rakʿahs, his reward is like that of a complete ḥajj and ʿumrah. [Sunan al-Tirmidhī, sound]

From all of this we understand what is meant by *mujāhadah* of the soul in the matter of *sahar*, and why *sahar* is a pillar of *mujāhadah*—with the observation that *sahar* itself is not a goal.

Wakefulness may be disliked: in the *ḥadīth*, "The Messenger of Allah used to dislike sleeping before ʿIshāʾ and engaging in conversation after it." [Sahīh al-Bukhārī]

Wakefulness accompanied by idle talk is disliked, so what if it is accompanied by the forbidden? As for wakefulness whose driving force is action, work, remembrance, *qiyām*, and Qur'an recitation, in which no congregational prayer is lost—such wakefulness is what is intended.

> The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, used to keep vigil with Abū Bakr, discussing the affairs of the Muslims. [Sahīh al-Bukhārī]

And it was from the *sunnah* of Dāwūd, peace be upon him: "He used to sleep half the night, stand in prayer for a third of it, then sleep a sixth of it." [Sahīh al-Bukhārī]

When we understand the issue of *sahar*, let us remember that sleep is a normal human need. When we call a person to *sahar*, we are calling him to accustom his soul to a complete Islamic life. Therefore, the Muslim must compensate his body's need for sleep at other times if he misses his share of it at night. For this reason the *qaylūlah* (midday nap) was part of the *sunnah*—a nap before the Ẓuhr prayer; one who misses it can compensate after the Ẓuhr prayer. The matter is broad, and with all of this we have understood the wisdom of this pillar of *mujāhadah*.

## Interconnection of the Pillars and Spiritual Retreats

It will be noticed that these four pillars are connected to one another. Whoever eats much needs much sleep. Whoever strives against his soul through silence may miss out on a night of vigil for himself. And solitude helps one control the matters of *sahar*, *ṣamt*, and food.

Perhaps through our presentation of the issue of the pillars of *mujāhadah* we have come to understand why these four matters are considered pillars of it. If a Muslim can control his speech, food, sleep, and companionship, he has reached the gates of all good, and has become able to control whatever lies beyond that. If a person organizes cycles in his life in which he regulates these matters, so that a disciplined state becomes normal in his life with lower and upper limits, that is the ordinary condition in the life of a Muslim.

Let us return to the idea of spiritual retreats to add to them the element of *mujāhadah*. Suppose I resolve to undertake for myself a spiritual and psychological retreat of forty days—and forty is not a condition, as we have seen previously. But there are many texts from which one may draw encouragement on this subject. Among them is Allah's saying:

> And We made an appointment with Moses for thirty nights and perfected them by [adding] ten; so the term of his Lord was completed as forty nights. [Qur'an, Surah al-Aʿrāf 7:142]

And among them is his saying, peace and blessings be upon him: "Whoever prays for forty days in congregation, catching the first *takbīr*, Allah will write for him two freedoms: freedom from the Fire and freedom from hypocrisy." [Sunan al-Tirmidhī, sound]

And among them is this narration: "A man said: O Messenger of Allah, how long should I fast? He said: Fast three days. He said: Can I fast more? He said: Fast six. He said: Can I fast more? He said: Fast ten. He said: Can I fast more? He said: Fast twenty. He said: Can I fast more? He said: Fast forty." [Sunan al-Nasāʾī, sound]

So forty is what one may draw encouragement from.

If I resolve to spend these forty days with the least amount of companionship, without neglecting duties; and I arrange my food so that I content myself with necessary morsels; and I arrange my *sahar* and sleep during the day so as to achieve the aims of *sahar* and sleep; and I arrange my speech so that I say only what is necessary—while arranging the matters of knowledge, prayer, fasting, devotional practices, and Qur'an recitation mentioned earlier—then by that I will have combined in this retreat various kinds of *mujāhadah* and treatment at the same time. If I arrange all of this along with the matter of commanding good and forbidding evil, or with the matter of calling to Allah, or with a military work, or military training, or with an intensive public program, then the retreat will yield a great return. It is possible for each issue to have a retreat devoted to it in particular while the other obligations remain.

If we miss arranging these matters through long retreats, let us arrange them in another way. And if we miss the matter of retreats along with full-time devotion, we can still arrange them alongside the work of daily life. What cannot be attained in full should not be abandoned in its entirety. The path to Paradise is hard and has a price.

> Indeed, the merchandise of Allah is precious. Indeed, the merchandise of Allah is Paradise. [Sunan al-Tirmidhī]
